Module 01 / 07

Predictive, Adaptive, Hybrid

Part 1 introduced the three life-cycle shapes in theory. Here's what they actually look like running: Scrum and Kanban as the two dominant adaptive frameworks, and how all seven domains from this series show up differently depending which shape you're in.

What you'll learn
  • The basic mechanics of Scrum and Kanban
  • How each of the 7 domains looks different under predictive vs. adaptive delivery
  • Why "we're doing Agile" is not, by itself, a complete answer to how a project runs

Scrum, Briefly

Scrum organizes work into fixed-length sprints (commonly one to four weeks): a planning session sets the sprint's scope from a prioritized backlog, daily short standups surface blockers, a sprint review demonstrates what shipped, and a retrospective captures what to improve next time. A Product Owner owns the backlog's priority; a Scrum Master owns the process and removes obstacles — deliberately separate from who owns technical delivery.

One sprint's internal cycle
This whole cycle recurs every sprint — recall Part 1, Module 3: all 5 Focus Areas (Initiating…Closing) happen inside every iteration, at a small scale.

Kanban, Briefly

Kanban skips fixed sprints in favor of continuous flow: work items move across a visual board (To Do → In Progress → Done, or finer stages), with WIP limits (work-in-progress caps per column) preventing overload. There's no sprint boundary — work is pulled as capacity frees up, not batched into fixed cycles.

It depends, still: "we're doing Agile" doesn't answer how governance, finance, or risk actually work on your project — those decisions still have to be made explicitly, just inside a different rhythm. Tailoring (Module 2) applies exactly as much to an adaptive project as a predictive one.

Tailoring Beyond Life Cycle

Choosing predictive/adaptive/hybrid is the biggest tailoring decision, but not the only one. The same "match the effort to the actual stakes" logic (Part 1) applies to: how formal the change control process needs to be (Part 3), how detailed the WBS needs to go (Part 4), whether EVM gets tracked weekly or monthly (Part 5), and how many stakeholders genuinely need "Manage Closely" attention versus "Monitor" (Part 6).

Good practice: write the tailoring decision down, with the reasoning, not just the conclusion. "We chose hybrid because construction has a hard permit gate but the platform's UX is genuinely undecided" is a decision someone can revisit and defend later — "we're doing hybrid" alone isn't.

RACI — the One This Series Hadn't Built Yet

A RACI matrix names, for each key decision or task, who's Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome — exactly one person), Consulted (gives input beforehand), and Informed (told after). It's not one of PMBOK 8's named artifacts, but it's a near-universal practical tool for exactly the kind of ambiguity Part 3's Governance domain exists to prevent.

Reading a Situational Question

PMP-style scenario questions typically ask what the PM should do FIRST, NEXT, or BEST. A practical technique:

  1. Identify what's actually being tested — which domain or principle does the scenario turn on, underneath the story details?
  2. Eliminate options that skip a step — an option that jumps straight to escalating or replanning, without first investigating or communicating, is usually wrong even if it's eventually the right move.
  3. Favor the proactive, leadership-oriented option — per Part 2's principles, PMI's own framing consistently rewards the PM who addresses root cause and empowers the team over the PM who dictates or avoids.
  4. Among plausible options, pick the most complete one — the option that addresses the underlying cause, not just the symptom described in the scenario.
Caveat, honestly stated: this technique is widely reported test-prep guidance, cross-consistent with how this series' own principles (Part 2) and processes are framed — but it is not a verified, official PMI statement about how questions are scored. Treat it as a useful heuristic, not a guarantee.

Module 05 / 07

Capstone Case Study

One project, every domain, start to finish. This is the Fairview Public Library → Community Tech Hub case that's threaded through Parts 3–6 — now assembled into the single continuous story it was always building toward.

What you'll learn
  • How all 7 domains actually interact across a single project's real timeline
  • Where each artifact and technique from this series shows up in one coherent story
  • What "the standard, applied" looks like end to end, not as 7 separate topics

The Full Story

A city Library Services director sponsors the renovation of the aging Fairview branch into a community tech hub — a maker-lab, digital-literacy classes, and a new membership platform — funded by city budget plus a $2.4M philanthropic grant with a hard 14-month deadline (Governance, Part 3, Module 1: charter and business case).

Requirements are elicited through interviews, workshops, and direct observation — including watching actual seniors struggle with a mock sign-up flow, surfacing the need for a paper fallback option (Scope, Part 4, Module 1). The WBS splits the work into Construction, Platform, and Programs branches (Part 4, Module 2), and a proposed unrequested dashboard widget gets caught and rejected as gold-plating during scope control (Part 4, Module 3).

Backend and Frontend platform builds run in parallel on the network diagram, with Integration Testing and Launch on the critical path — 25 days end to end — while a PERT estimate covers Integration Testing's uncertainty (Part 4, Modules 4–5). Construction is contracted fixed-price; platform development runs cost-reimbursable given genuinely evolving scope (Governance, Part 3, Module 3).

At a mid-project check-in, Earned Value Management shows CPI 0.91 and SPI 0.83 — over budget and behind schedule (Finance, Part 5, Module 3) — triggering fast-tracking on a soft dependency to recover schedule (Part 4, Module 6) and a specialist's resource leveling that pushes the platform's finish date by a few days (Resources, Part 5, Module 4). The team itself moves through visible Storming over design authority in week two, resolved through deliberate Collaborate-style conflict work rather than the PM dictating a decision (Part 5, Modules 5–6).

Meanwhile, the power/interest grid keeps the philanthropic funder in "Manage Closely" and neighboring businesses in "Keep Informed" — until construction noise complaints push them toward Resistant, triggering an adjusted engagement plan (Stakeholders, Part 6, Modules 1–3). The Risk Breakdown Structure organizes identified risks across four categories; the 5×5 matrix prioritizes a possible budget overrun over a low-turnout risk; and an EMV analysis on a sourcing decision favors the guaranteed fixed-price option ($80,000) over a riskier alternative (EMV $82,000) (Risk, Part 6, Modules 4–5). The permit-delay risk — Mitigated by an early application with buffer — carries a week-6 monitoring trigger that never fires (Part 6, Module 6).

A mid-project change request (an added accessibility feature) goes through the CCB, gets approved, and updates the baseline formally rather than sliding in as scope creep (Governance, Part 3, Module 5). At month 14, the renovated library and new platform are formally accepted, contracts are closed, the team disbands (their Adjourning, per Tuckman), a lessons-learned session captures what to do differently next time, and library operations staff receive a documented handover (Governance, Part 3, Module 6).
