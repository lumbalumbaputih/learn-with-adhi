Module 01 / 07

Cost Estimating

Every number in this part traces back to an estimate of what each work package will cost. Three techniques trade accuracy against effort, and knowing which one you're using — and being honest about its precision — matters more than the number itself.

What you'll learn
  • The three main cost-estimating techniques, and the accuracy/effort tradeoff between them
  • What a cost baseline is, and why it has to be time-phased, not just a single total
  • Why the estimating technique should match how much is actually known yet

Three Estimating Techniques

TechniqueHow it worksTradeoff
AnalogousCompares to a similar past project's actual cost, scaled for size/complexityFast, low effort — but only as accurate as the analogy really is
ParametricCost = rate × quantity (cost per square meter × area; cost per hour × hours)More accurate than analogous when the rate is well established, still fast
Bottom-upEstimate each work package individually, sum them into the totalMost accurate — and by far the most time-consuming
Accuracy vs. effort — the tradeoff
More effort buys more accuracy — but bottom-up on every single line item of a small project can cost more (in time) than the precision is worth.

The Cost Baseline

Individual estimates get aggregated by work package and time-phased into a cost baseline — an approved budget spread across the project's timeline, not just one final number. Time-phasing matters because it's what makes Module 3's Earned Value comparison possible: you need to know what should have been spent by a given date, not just what the total is allowed to be.

It depends, on project stage: analogous estimates are normal (and honest) early, when little is known. As the WBS and requirements firm up, shifting to bottom-up for at least the highest-risk or highest-cost work packages is worth the extra effort — you don't need bottom-up precision on every line, just the ones that matter most.

A team estimates a new platform build by comparing it to a similar system built two years ago, scaled up for size. Which technique is this?

Module 02 / 07

Budgeting & the S-Curve

Spending on a project is never a straight line — it starts slow, accelerates through the middle, and tapers at the end. That shape has a name, and reserves sit above it for exactly the situations a baseline can't predict.

What you'll learn
  • Why cumulative project spending forms an S-shaped curve
  • The difference between contingency reserve and management reserve
  • Why confusing the two reserves causes real governance problems

Why Spending Forms an S-Curve

Early in a project, only planning and setup activities are running — spending is low. As execution ramps up, multiple work packages run in parallel and cumulative spending climbs steeply. As the project approaches closure, fewer activities remain and spending tapers off. Plotted cumulatively over time, this produces a recognizable S shape — flat, then steep, then flat again.

Cumulative budget over time, with reserves above the baseline
The baseline is what's planned; contingency sits just above it for known risks within the PM's control; management reserve sits highest, for unknowns the PM typically can't tap without sponsor approval.

Two Kinds of Reserve

  • Contingency reserve — set aside for identified risks that made it into the risk register (Part 6 covers risk in depth). "We know a permit delay is plausible; we've budgeted for it." Usually within the project manager's authority to use.
  • Management reserve — set aside for the genuinely unknown-unknowns, things nobody identified as a specific risk. Typically held above the project budget, released only with sponsor or governance approval (Part 3's governance layer, in practice).
The trap: a PM who quietly spends management reserve without escalating, treating it like their own contingency fund. Beyond being a governance violation (Part 3), it removes the sponsor's visibility into how much true risk buffer is left — exactly the information they need for their own next-level portfolio decisions (Part 1).
Quick Check – Module 2

Module 03 / 07

Earned Value Management

The single most exam-tested technique in project management — and genuinely useful, because it's the only method here that measures cost and schedule performance together, in a way "we're 60% through the timeline" alone never can.

What you'll learn
  • The three core EVM numbers — Planned Value, Earned Value, Actual Cost
  • How to calculate and interpret cost/schedule variance and performance indexes
  • How to forecast a final cost using Estimate at Completion

Three Numbers, One Snapshot

  • PV (Planned Value) — the budgeted cost of the work that should have been done by now, per the baseline.
  • EV (Earned Value) — the budgeted cost of the work actually completed by now. Not what was spent — what was earned, valued at its planned cost.
  • AC (Actual Cost) — what was actually spent to get that work done.

All three numbers matter together. PV alone tells you the plan. AC alone tells you spending. Only EV lets you compare "value of work done" against both of the others — which is exactly what makes cost and schedule performance measurable in the same terms.

Worked Example: the Fairview Platform Build

At a check-in, the platform work package has a Budget at Completion (BAC) of $100,000:

$60,000
PV — value of work planned to be done by now
$50,000
EV — value of work actually completed
$55,000
AC — actual money spent so far
MetricFormulaResultReading
Cost Variance (CV)EV − AC50,000 − 55,000 = −$5,000Over budget for the work completed
Schedule Variance (SV)EV − PV50,000 − 60,000 = −$10,000Behind schedule
Cost Performance Index (CPI)EV ÷ AC50,000 ÷ 55,000 ≈ 0.91Earning only $0.91 of value per $1 spent
Schedule Performance Index (SPI)EV ÷ PV50,000 ÷ 60,000 ≈ 0.83Progressing at 83% of the planned pace
Estimate at Completion (EAC)BAC ÷ CPI100,000 ÷ 0.909 = $110,000If this cost trend continues, expect to finish $10,000 over the original budget
Estimate to Complete (ETC)EAC − AC110,000 − 55,000 = $55,000Roughly this much more needs to be spent to finish
PV, EV, and AC — one snapshot, three bars
EV below PV = behind schedule. EV below AC = over budget for what's actually been earned. Both are true here at once.
Reading the story, not just the numbers: CPI < 1 and SPI < 1 together tell a specific story — the team is spending more than the value it's producing, and producing that value more slowly than planned. That's a worse position than either problem alone, and it's the kind of pattern a single "% complete" figure would never surface.

Module 04 / 07

Resource Planning

A schedule that assumes a specialist is available 150% of the time isn't a schedule — it's a wish. Resource planning is where the network diagram from Part 4 meets the reality of who's actually free when.

What you'll learn
  • What resource planning actually has to account for beyond headcount
  • The difference between resource leveling and resource smoothing
  • Why over-allocation is often invisible until you actually check

Planning Beyond Headcount

Resource planning identifies what each activity needs — not just "a developer," but which skillset, for how long, and when they're actually available (accounting for other project commitments, leave, and organizational calendars). This is where Part 3's integration discipline gets tested directly: a schedule and a resource plan that were written without talking to each other will conflict, exactly as Part 3, Module 2 warned.

Resource Leveling vs. Resource Smoothing

What it doesEffect on finish date
Resource levelingAdjusts the schedule (start/end dates) to resolve over-allocationCan push the finish date out
Resource smoothingAdjusts activities only within their existing floatNever changes the finish date — but can only resolve over-allocation the float allows
Before and after leveling
Week 2 (marked *) exceeded capacity before leveling. After leveling, the same total work fits under the line — but takes longer to finish.
Why over-allocation hides: a schedule built from a network diagram alone (Part 4) can look perfectly reasonable — every dependency respected, every duration realistic — while quietly assuming one person is 130% booked for a week. Nothing in the diagram itself flags it. Only a deliberate resource check catches it.

Module 05 / 07

Acquiring & Building the Team

Assembling a team is the easy part. Turning a group of individuals into a team that actually performs well together follows a predictable, and mostly unavoidable, sequence.

What you'll learn
  • The main ways a team gets acquired (pre-assignment, negotiation, virtual teams)
  • Tuckman's five stages of team development
  • Why "storming" is a normal, necessary stage — not a sign something's broken

Acquiring the Team

Team members arrive by a few common routes: pre-assignment (named in the charter or contract before the project even starts), negotiation (the PM negotiates with functional managers for specific people — recall Part 1's org-structure spectrum, since this negotiation looks very different in a functional org vs. a projectized one), and increasingly, virtual teams spread across locations, which need deliberate extra attention to communication (Part 6 covers this in depth).

Tuckman's Stages of Team Development

Team performance across the five stages
The dip at Storming is normal — conflict over roles, working style, and authority surfaces here, and skipping past it rarely works.
StageWhat's happening
FormingPolite, cautious. People are figuring out the team and their place in it.
StormingConflict surfaces — over roles, working style, authority. Uncomfortable, but necessary.
NormingThe team settles into working agreements and starts trusting each other.
PerformingHigh trust, high autonomy — the team operates effectively with minimal oversight.
AdjourningThe team disbands at project closure (Part 3, Module 6) — a real transition, not just an administrative event.
Good practice: a leader who recognizes Storming for what it is — normal friction, not a crisis — and creates space to work through it (structured retrospectives, explicit role clarification) moves a team through it faster than one who either suppresses the conflict or panics at the first sign of it.

Module 06 / 07

Leading the Team

Part 2 argued leadership is a behavior, not a title. This module gets practical about it: what actually motivates people, how to handle conflict without dodging it, and keeping an eye on whether resourcing is holding up as planned.

What you'll learn
  • Three classic motivation theories, and the one practical lesson each contributes
  • Five conflict-resolution styles, mapped by assertiveness and cooperativeness
  • What "monitoring resourcing" means day to day

Motivation Theory, Briefly

🔺

Maslow's Hierarchy

People need lower needs (safety, belonging) reasonably met before higher ones (esteem, growth) motivate them well. A team worried about job security won't respond much to "growth opportunity" framing.

⚖️

Herzberg's Two-Factor

Hygiene factors (fair pay, safe conditions) prevent dissatisfaction but don't motivate on their own. Motivators (meaningful work, recognition, growth) are what actually drive engagement.

🔄

McGregor's Theory X/Y

Theory X assumes people avoid work and need control. Theory Y assumes people are self-motivated given the right conditions. A leader's assumption becomes somewhat self-fulfilling — ties directly to Part 2's Empowered Culture.

Conflict-Resolution Styles

Five styles, by assertiveness and cooperativeness
Collaborate (high on both) usually produces the best outcome but costs the most time — knowing when Compromise or even Avoid is the right call for a low-stakes issue is part of the skill.
StyleBest for
AvoidTrivial issues, or when emotions need to cool before engaging
AccommodateWhen the relationship matters more than this particular issue
CompeteUrgent decisions where there's no time for consensus
CompromiseBoth sides have valid, roughly equal claims and time is limited
CollaborateHigh-stakes issues where a genuinely better solution exists if both sides dig in together

Monitoring Resourcing

Leading the team isn't only about people dynamics — it includes watching whether the resource plan (Module 4) is still holding up: is anyone quietly over-allocated again, has someone's availability changed, does the team's actual capacity still match what the schedule assumes. This closes the loop back to Part 3's monitoring discipline, applied specifically to people.
