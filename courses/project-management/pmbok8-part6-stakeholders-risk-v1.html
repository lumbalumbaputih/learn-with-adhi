Module 01 / 07

Identifying Stakeholders

A stakeholder is anyone who can affect the project or be affected by it — a wider net than "the people on the team." Missing one doesn't make them disappear; it just means you meet them later, usually at a worse moment.

What you'll learn
  • How to identify stakeholders systematically, not just the obvious ones
  • The power/interest grid, and a matching strategy for each quadrant
  • Why stakeholder analysis has to be revisited, not done once at kickoff

Finding Stakeholders Systematically

Beyond the obvious (sponsor, team, customer), systematic identification checks: who has budget authority nearby, who's affected by the project's side effects (noise, disruption, changed processes), who has regulatory or oversight power, and who was a stakeholder on a similar past project. A quick technique: walk the org chart and the neighborhood, not just the requirements document.

The Power/Interest Grid

Once identified, not every stakeholder needs the same attention. Plotting each on two axes — power to affect the project, and interest in its outcome — turns a long list into four clear engagement strategies.

Power/interest grid — Fairview library case
The funder (high power + high interest) gets weekly attention. Neighboring businesses (low power, high interest — the renovation disrupts their foot traffic) get kept informed so frustration doesn't escalate into real opposition.
It depends, and it's dynamic: a stakeholder's position on this grid isn't fixed. A neighboring business that starts as "Keep Informed" can become "Manage Closely" fast if construction noise triggers a formal complaint to the city council — reassess the grid at major milestones, not just once at kickoff.
Quick Check – Module 1

A local news outlet has no formal power over the project but strong interest in covering it. Midway through, they publish a critical story that pressures the city council. What does this suggest about "low power" stakeholders?

Why does the module recommend reassessing the power/interest grid "at major milestones," not just once at kickoff?

Module 02 / 07

Engagement & Comms Planning

Knowing where a stakeholder sits on the grid is only step one. This module plans two related but distinct things: how to move a stakeholder's actual engagement, and how information physically flows so the right people know the right things.

What you'll learn
  • How to assess a stakeholder's current vs. desired engagement level
  • What a communications management plan actually specifies
  • The communication-channels formula, and why team size matters more than people expect

Current vs. Desired Engagement

Beyond power and interest, it helps to name where a stakeholder actually stands today, and where you need them to be: Unaware → Resistant → Neutral → Supportive → Leading. The gap between current and desired is what an engagement plan is actually trying to close.

Closing the engagement gap — neighboring businesses
Neighboring businesses start Resistant (worried about lost foot traffic); the realistic near-term target is Neutral, not Leading — an honest, achievable gap.

The Communications Management Plan

Where the engagement plan is strategy, the communications plan is mechanics: for each audience, what information, in what format, through which channel, how often, and who's responsible for sending it. A sponsor's weekly status email and a construction crew's daily safety briefing are both "communication," but they're entirely different plans.

The channels formula: the number of potential communication channels in a group of n people is n(n − 1) ÷ 2. A 6-person core team has 6 × 5 ÷ 2 = 15 potential channels. Add 4 more people (to 10) and it jumps to 10 × 9 ÷ 2 = 45 — team size grows communication complexity much faster than people intuitively expect, which is exactly why larger teams need more deliberate communication structure, not just more meetings.
Quick Check – Module 2

A project team grows from 8 people to 12. Using n(n−1)÷2, roughly how much do potential communication channels increase?

Why does the module distinguish "engagement level" (Unaware…Leading) from the power/interest grid covered in Module 1?

A communications plan says "the sponsor gets a weekly status email," but doesn't specify format, channel, or who's responsible for sending it. What's missing?

Module 03 / 07

Managing & Monitoring

A plan is not the same as the thing actually happening. This module covers executing the engagement and communications plans for real, and checking — deliberately, per Part 3's monitoring discipline — whether they're actually working.

What you'll learn
  • The difference between sending a message and it actually landing
  • What "managing" engagement looks like day to day
  • How to monitor whether engagement and communication are working

Sent ≠ Received ≠ Understood

Communication has a sender, a message, a channel, a receiver, and — critically — noise that can distort it along the way: jargon the receiver doesn't share, a channel they don't check, a moment they're distracted. A status email being sent is not the same as it being read, and being read is not the same as being understood the way you intended.

The communication model, with noise and feedback
Without the feedback loop, a sender has no way of knowing whether "sent" ever became "understood."

Managing Engagement, Day to Day

Managing engagement means actively working relationships: addressing a stakeholder's concerns before they harden into opposition, clarifying expectations before a misunderstanding compounds, and adjusting tactics as a stakeholder's position shifts (Module 1's dynamic grid). It's relationship work, not a one-time send-and-forget plan execution.

Monitoring: Is It Actually Working?

Monitoring checks the plan against reality: is a target stakeholder's engagement level actually moving toward the desired state (Module 2), or stuck? Are status reports landing with the right people, or getting buried? Recall Part 3's core monitoring lesson — comparing plan to actual on a defined cadence — applied here to relationships and information flow instead of schedule and cost.

The trap: treating "we sent the weekly update" as proof engagement is on track. A perfectly executed communications plan can still fail if nobody's actually reading it, or if what's being sent isn't what a resistant stakeholder actually needs to hear to move toward Neutral.
Quick Check – Module 3

How does "managing engagement" differ from simply executing the communications plan on schedule?

A resistant stakeholder's engagement level hasn't moved toward Neutral despite weeks of scheduled updates. What does the monitoring discipline from Part 3 suggest here?

Module 04 / 07

Risk Planning & Identification

A risk that isn't written down doesn't stop existing — it just stops being managed. This module is about finding risks systematically, before they find you, and organizing them so nothing important gets lost in a disorganized list.

What you'll learn
  • What a risk management plan sets up before identification even starts
  • Techniques for identifying risk systematically
  • How a Risk Breakdown Structure organizes risks by source

Planning Before Identifying

Before hunting for risks, a risk management plan sets the ground rules: how risk will be categorized, who's involved in identifying and responding to it, and what threshold makes a risk worth tracking formally versus ignoring as noise. Skipping this means every risk conversation re-litigates its own rules.

Identifying Risk

  • Brainstorming and workshops — pull knowledge out of the people closest to the work, same as Part 4's requirements elicitation.
  • Checklists — from past, similar projects; fast, but only catches what's been seen before.
  • Assumption analysis — every plan rests on assumptions; testing which ones are shaky surfaces risk directly.
  • Reviewing past projects — what actually went wrong last time, not just what was worried about.

The Risk Breakdown Structure

A Risk Breakdown Structure (RBS) organizes identified risks hierarchically by source — the same decomposition logic as the WBS (Part 4), applied to where risk comes from instead of what work needs doing.

Risk Breakdown Structure — Fairview project
Same 100%-coverage discipline as the WBS: every identified risk should trace to a category, and the categories should cover the realistic universe of risk sources.
Connects to Part 5: once identified, a risk gets a place in the risk register — the living document that ultimately justifies the contingency reserve (Part 5, Module 2). Identification and budgeting aren't separate conversations; they feed each other directly.

Module 05 / 07

Risk Analysis & EMV

Not every identified risk deserves the same response effort. Qualitative analysis sorts risks fast, by feel; quantitative analysis — specifically Expected Monetary Value — puts an actual number on a decision, and sometimes flips what "feels right" on its head.

What you'll learn
  • How a probability/impact matrix prioritizes risks quickly
  • The Expected Monetary Value formula, worked through a real decision
  • Why EMV can favor a "safe-looking" option that isn't actually cheapest, or vice versa

Qualitative Analysis: The Probability/Impact Matrix

5×5 probability/impact matrix, Fairview risks plotted
Fast triage: "Budget Overrun" (moderate-high probability, high impact) demands more attention right now than "Low Turnout" (high probability, low impact) — even though the latter is more likely to happen at all.

Quantitative Analysis: Expected Monetary Value

Expected Monetary Value (EMV) multiplies each possible outcome's probability by its dollar impact, then sums across outcomes — turning "how risky does this feel" into an actual comparable number.

$80,000
Option A — fixed-price contractor, guaranteed cost, no variance
60% · $70,000
Option B — favorable outcome probability & cost
40% · $100,000
Option B — unfavorable outcome probability & cost
Decision tree — Option A vs. Option B
Option B's EMV = (0.6 × $70,000) + (0.4 × $100,000) = $42,000 + $40,000 = $82,000 — actually $2,000 more, on average, than Option A's guaranteed $80,000. The "flexible" option isn't automatically the cheaper one.
The real lesson: EMV doesn't tell you what will happen on this specific project — it tells you what the average outcome would be if you faced this exact decision many times. A risk-averse sponsor might still prefer Option A's certainty even if an even-cheaper Option B existed; EMV informs the decision, it doesn't automatically make it.

Module 06 / 07

Risk Response

Risk isn't only downside. PMBOK has long treated "positive risk" — opportunity — as a mirror category deserving its own strategies, not just an afterthought to threat management. This module covers both, plus the ongoing discipline of watching them.

What you'll learn
  • Four response strategies for threats, and four mirror strategies for opportunities
  • How to choose the right strategy for a given risk
  • What monitoring risk actually involves after a response is chosen

Four Strategies Each, Mirrored

Threat strategies and their opportunity mirror
Every threat strategy has a genuine positive-risk mirror — "Transfer" a threat via insurance or a fixed-price contract; "Share" an opportunity via a joint venture that splits the upside too.
Threat strategyExample
AvoidChange the plan to remove the risk entirely — e.g. redesign to eliminate a permit-triggering feature
MitigateReduce probability or impact — e.g. submit the permit application earlier, with buffer
TransferShift the risk to another party — e.g. a fixed-price contract (Part 3) shifts cost-overrun risk to the vendor
AcceptDo nothing proactive, but budget the contingency reserve (Part 5) for it if it happens

Monitoring Risk

Choosing a response isn't the end. Monitoring means watching for a risk's trigger conditions, checking whether the response is actually working, reviewing whether identified risks are still relevant, and — just as important — catching new risks that emerge as the project proceeds. A risk register frozen at kickoff is already going stale by the time execution really gets underway.

Good practice, Fairview case: the permit-delay risk (Mitigate: submit early with buffer) gets a monitoring trigger — "if approval hasn't come through by week 6, escalate to the city relationship contact" — rather than just hoping it resolves. A named trigger turns a vague worry into an actionable check.
Quick Check – Module 6
