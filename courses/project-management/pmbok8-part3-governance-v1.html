Module 01 / 07

Starting a Project

Governance is the biggest domain in PMBOK 8 — old "Integration Management" territory, the glue that holds the other six domains together. It starts here: turning an idea into a formally authorized project with a named owner and a defined boundary.

What you'll learn
  • What a business case actually has to justify
  • What a project charter must contain to do its job
  • Why "authorized" is a specific, meaningful word — not a formality

The Business Case

Before a project exists, someone has to justify why it should — and, per Part 1's portfolio logic, why it deserves funding over the other candidates competing for the same budget. A business case names the problem or opportunity, the options considered (including "do nothing"), and the expected value against the cost. It's the document a portfolio decision-maker actually reads before saying yes.

The Charter: From Idea to Authorized Project

A project charter is the document that formally authorizes a project to exist and gives the project manager the authority to apply organizational resources to it. Before the charter is signed, there is no project — just a proposal. After, there's a named sponsor, a defined (if high-level) scope, and a mandate.

From idea to authorized project
Only the last step gives the PM real authority — everything before it is still a proposal, however good the idea.

What a charter must contain

  • Purpose and justification — the "why," pulled from the business case
  • High-level scope and deliverables — what's in, roughly, and what's explicitly out
  • Named sponsor and project manager — who's accountable, who's authorized to act
  • Success criteria — how "done, and done well" will be judged
  • Key assumptions and constraints — the ground rules the plan will be built on
  • High-level budget and timeline range — not the detailed plan yet, just the envelope
Fairview case: the library's charter names the city's Library Services director as sponsor, sets a $2.4M budget envelope and a hard 14-month deadline tied to the grant, and states success as "renovated facility operational, membership platform live, digital-literacy program running" — without yet specifying exactly which vendor, which construction sequence, or which platform features. That detail comes later, once planning begins in each domain.
The trap: starting real work — hiring, spending, committing vendors — before the charter is signed. It feels efficient ("we're confident this will get approved") but it means work is happening with no one formally authorized to direct it, no defined budget ceiling, and no agreed success criteria. If it goes wrong, there's no charter to point to for what was actually promised.
Quick Check – Module 1

A team starts hiring contractors and spending budget on a project idea that hasn't been formally chartered yet, because "everyone agrees it's a good idea." What's the risk?

A charter states a $2.4M budget envelope and a 14-month deadline, but doesn't specify which construction vendor will be used or the exact sequence of work. Is this charter incomplete?

Why does a business case need to consider "do nothing" as one of its options, alongside the proposed project?

Module 02 / 07

Integration Across Plans

Part 2's "Adopt a Holistic View" was a mindset. This module is that mindset made mechanical: every domain writes its own subsidiary plan, and someone has to keep all of them pointed at the same project.

What you'll learn
  • Why every domain produces its own subsidiary plan, and why that's not redundant
  • How the project management plan integrates them into one coherent whole
  • What breaks when integration is skipped

Six Plans, One Project

Scope planning produces a scope management plan. Schedule planning produces a schedule management plan. The same is true for finance, resources, stakeholders, and risk (Parts 4–6 cover each in depth). Written in isolation, these plans will contradict each other — the schedule assumes resources the resource plan hasn't actually secured, the budget assumes a scope the requirements aren't finalized on yet. Integration is the ongoing work of reconciling them into one internally consistent project management plan.

One integrated plan, six subsidiary inputs
Each subsidiary plan is written by a different lens (Parts 4–6 cover them) — integration is what keeps them from contradicting each other as the project evolves.

Integration Is Ongoing, Not a One-Time Merge

Integration isn't a single step where six documents get stapled together at the start. It's continuous: every time one subsidiary plan changes — a schedule slip, a budget cut, a new risk — integration is the work of checking what else needs to change to stay consistent. This is the direct, day-to-day expression of Part 2's Holistic View principle: the discipline of always asking "what else does this touch?"

What skipping this looks like: the schedule team commits to a four-week platform build sprint. Nobody tells the resource plan, which still shows the two senior developers allocated to a different internal project during that exact window. Both plans are individually "correct." The project isn't.
Quick Check – Module 2

A schedule change is approved without checking whether the budget, staffing, or risk plans need to change too. What has failed?

Why does the module insist integration is "ongoing, not a one-time merge"?

Module 03 / 07

Sourcing & Procurement

Most real projects buy something — a contractor, a vendor, a specialist consultant. Which contract type you choose isn't a legal footnote; it decides who eats the cost if the work runs over.

What you'll learn
  • The make-or-buy decision, and what actually drives it
  • The three major contract types, and who bears cost-overrun risk under each
  • Why matching contract type to scope certainty matters more than getting the lowest quoted price

Make or Buy?

Before choosing a contract type, decide whether to build a capability in-house or acquire it externally. The honest drivers are usually: does this capability need to exist permanently inside the organization, or just for this project; is it cheaper to build the skill or rent it; and how much control does the project need over how the work gets done. The Fairview library case makes both calls: the city keeps the community-program design in-house (it needs to persist after the project), and buys the construction and specialized platform development externally (temporary need, specialized skill).

Three Contract Types, Three Risk Allocations

FP
Fixed-Price — the seller bears the cost-overrun risk. Best when scope is well defined.
e.g. construction contract
CR
Cost-Reimbursable — the buyer bears the cost-overrun risk. Best when scope is genuinely uncertain.
e.g. R&D-heavy work
T&M
Time & Materials — risk is shared, usually capped by a not-to-exceed clause.
e.g. short, evolving engagements
Where cost-overrun risk lands
Matching contract type to actual scope certainty avoids the worst outcome: the wrong party holding a risk they can't control.
The classic mistake: forcing a fixed-price contract onto genuinely uncertain, evolving work (a research-heavy software build) to "control cost." The vendor either pads the price heavily to cover their risk, or cuts corners when reality diverges from the fixed scope — because they, not the buyer, now eat any overrun. Fixed-price only controls cost when the scope really is well defined.

Module 04 / 07

Executing & Quality Assurance

Planning is where you decide what to do. Executing is where you actually direct and manage the work — and where Part 2's "Embed Quality" principle turns into a repeatable habit, not a one-off inspection.

What you'll learn
  • What "directing and managing project work" actually involves day to day
  • The Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle as a practical embedding technique
  • Where quality assurance activities fit inside execution, not after it

Directing and Managing the Work

Executing is where the project management plan becomes real activity: assigning work, removing blockers, coordinating across the team, integrating vendor deliverables, and keeping the team oriented toward the plan as reality inevitably deviates from it slightly, every day.

Plan-Do-Check-Act: Quality as a Habit, Not an Event

The Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle — a long-standing quality-management technique, not a PMI invention — is a simple, repeatable loop for embedding quality during execution rather than inspecting for it afterward: plan a small improvement or standard, do it, check whether it worked, act on what you learned by adjusting the standard. Run continuously, in small loops, instead of once at the end.

The Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle
A small, continuous loop — not one big quality event at the end of the project.

Quality Assurance During Execution

Quality assurance activities — process audits, peer reviews, standards checks — belong inside execution, running alongside the work, not scheduled as a separate phase after it. Waiting until "the quality phase" to check anything is exactly the inspection-heavy pattern Part 2's cost-of-quality diagram argued against.

Good practice, Fairview case: the construction team runs a short PDCA loop on the first renovated room before proceeding to the rest: plan the finishing technique, do one room, check it against the accessibility and durability standard, act on what's learned before repeating it across the remaining nine rooms — catching a fixable process issue on room one instead of all ten.
Quick Check – Module 4

A team schedules "the quality phase" as a dedicated block at the very end of the project, after all the building work is done. What does this module say about that plan?

A construction crew tests their finishing technique on one room, evaluates it against a standard, and adjusts before repeating it across nine more rooms. Which PDCA step have they just completed by evaluating room one?

Why is PDCA described as a "small, continuous loop" rather than a single big quality event?

Module 05 / 07

Change Control & Monitoring

Plans change. That's not a failure — pretending they won't is. What matters is whether change flows through a deliberate process or happens by accident, one quiet exception at a time.

What you'll learn
  • The integrated change control process, step by step
  • The four categories a change request can fall into
  • Why "monitoring" isn't just watching — it's comparing plan to reality on purpose

The Integrated Change Control Process

A change doesn't get absorbed into the project silently. It goes through a process: someone raises a change request, its impact on scope, schedule, cost, and risk gets assessed, a decision-making body (often called a Change Control Board, or CCB) approves or rejects it, and — if approved — the relevant baselines get formally updated.

The change control process
A rejected change isn't necessarily gone forever — it can return later with new information, but it doesn't get quietly absorbed without going through the process.

Four kinds of change

TypeWhat it addresses
Corrective actionRealigns work already underway that's drifted from the plan
Preventive actionReduces the likelihood of a future deviation before it happens
Defect repairFixes a nonconforming deliverable
Baseline updateFormally revises the approved scope, schedule, or cost baseline itself

Monitoring: Comparing Plan to Reality, on Purpose

Monitoring isn't passive observation — it's the deliberate act of comparing actual performance against the plan, on a defined cadence, so drift gets caught while it's still small and cheap to correct (echoing Module 4's PDCA logic). Part 5 covers the specific technique — Earned Value Management — used to quantify exactly how far off track a project is, in both cost and schedule terms.

It depends, on the change's size: not every change needs a full CCB session — a project should tailor change-control formality to the change's blast radius, the same way Part 1 argued for tailoring the life cycle itself. A one-line copy fix doesn't need the same process as a scope-baseline change.
Quick Check – Module 5

A team member quietly adds a requested feature without running it through change control, reasoning "it's small, no one will mind." What's the risk, even if the feature itself is harmless?

A team catches work that's drifted from the schedule baseline and takes action to realign it with the original plan. What type of change is this?

Module 06 / 07

Closing a Project

Recall Part 1: a project can legitimately end because the goal was achieved, proven impossible, or made unnecessary. Whichever it is, closing isn't just "stop working" — it's a defined set of activities that turns loose ends into a clean handover.

What you'll learn
  • What formal closure actually involves, beyond finishing the last task
  • Why lessons learned belong in the closing sequence, not as an afterthought
  • How closure connects back to operations — the value delivery system's next stage

The Closing Sequence

From final deliverable to archive
Skipping any step leaves a loose end — an unclosed contract, an unreleased resource still nominally "on the project," a lesson nobody captured.
  • Formal acceptance — the sponsor or customer explicitly confirms the deliverable meets the agreed success criteria (Module 1's charter is the reference point).
  • Contract closure — final payments settled, contractual obligations formally discharged with every vendor.
  • Resource release — team members and equipment are freed for their next assignment, not left in limbo.
  • Lessons learned — what worked, what didn't, captured while memory is fresh — not written from scratch months later when nobody remembers the details.
  • Archiving — project records stored where the organization (and future projects) can actually find them.

Closure Hands Off to Operations

Recall Part 1's value delivery system: the project's deliverable becomes a product that transitions into operations, which sustain it long after the project team disbands. Closure is the formal handoff point — and a rushed or skipped closure often means operations inherits something nobody properly explained to them.

Fairview case: closure means the renovated library and new platform are formally accepted by the city, the construction and IT vendor contracts are closed out, the temporary project staff roll off, a lessons-learned session captures what the next city renovation project should do differently, and library operations staff receive a documented handover — not just a finished building and an unexplained login screen.
