Module 01 / 07

Requirements

Wait — no accent word this time. Everything downstream in this part depends on getting this one right: the WBS, the schedule, even the budget in Part 5, all trace back to what was actually required, not what someone assumed was obvious.

What you'll learn
  • The main techniques for eliciting requirements, and when to use each
  • Functional vs. non-functional requirements — and why missing the second kind is the more common failure
  • Why traceability matters once a project is underway

Eliciting Requirements

Requirements don't arrive fully formed — they have to be actively drawn out, and different techniques surface different kinds of need.

TechniqueBest for
InterviewsDeep, individual insight — especially from a stakeholder who won't speak up in a group
WorkshopsFast convergence when multiple stakeholders need to agree in real time
SurveysBroad input from a large or dispersed group, at the cost of depth
ObservationWhat people actually do, not what they say they do — catches needs people don't think to mention
PrototypingReactions to something concrete, when people struggle to describe what they want in the abstract
Fairview case: the "seniors need a paper fallback for sign-up, not everyone has an email" requirement from Part 2's Embed Quality example wasn't volunteered in a workshop — it surfaced through observation, watching actual seniors try the mock-up. No interview or survey question happened to ask about it directly.

Functional vs. Non-Functional Requirements

Functional requirements describe what the system or deliverable does — "members can search the catalog," "the sign-up form collects a name and address." Non-functional requirements describe how well it does it, or under what constraints — response time, accessibility standards, how many concurrent users it must support, language support. Functional requirements are usually easy to elicit because stakeholders describe them naturally. Non-functional requirements are the ones that get missed — and missing them is what produces a system that technically works but fails in practice.

From broad stakeholder needs to defined requirements
The narrowing isn't loss — it's the deliberate work of turning broad input into something specific enough to design and build against.

Traceability

A requirements traceability matrix links each requirement to the deliverable(s) that satisfy it, and later to the test or acceptance criteria that confirm it. Its real value shows up mid-project: when a scope change is proposed, tracing which requirements it affects (Part 3's change control) turns a vague debate into a specific, checkable list.

Quick Check – Module 1

A stakeholder says the new platform must "support many people at once during peak enrollment week." Functional or non-functional requirement?

A team relies entirely on a stakeholder survey to gather requirements for a new physical space's accessibility needs, and misses a mobility issue that only becomes obvious when watching someone actually navigate the space. What technique would likely have caught it?

Midway through the project, someone proposes removing a feature. Why does a requirements traceability matrix make that conversation easier?

Module 02 / 07

Defining Scope & the WBS

Requirements describe needs. Scope turns them into a defined boundary — what's in, what's explicitly out — and the Work Breakdown Structure turns that boundary into something you can actually plan, schedule, and assign.

What you'll learn
  • What a scope statement has to nail down, including what's excluded
  • How to build a WBS by progressive decomposition
  • The 100% rule, and why it's the WBS's single most important discipline

The Scope Statement

A scope statement translates the charter's high-level boundary (Part 3, Module 1) into specifics: the deliverables, the acceptance criteria for each, and — just as important — explicit exclusions. "We are not renovating the parking structure" prevents a whole category of scope-creep argument three months later.

Building the WBS

The Work Breakdown Structure decomposes the total scope into progressively smaller pieces, until you reach work packages — pieces small enough to estimate, assign, and track reliably. Each level breaks its parent down further; nothing is invented at a lower level that wasn't already implied above it.

WBS — Fairview Community Tech Hub
Six work packages, three phases, one project — every branch narrower than its parent, nothing added that the level above didn't already imply.

The 100% Rule

A correctly built WBS captures 100% of the project's scope — no more, no less — at every level. A child node's combined scope must fully account for its parent's scope; nothing extra is smuggled in at a lower level, and nothing from the parent is left uncovered. This single discipline is what makes the WBS trustworthy as the foundation for the schedule (Modules 4–6) and the budget (Part 5).

The trap: a work package that "sounds reasonable" but wasn't actually implied by any requirement or scope statement item. That's scope invented at the WBS level — a preview of the "gold-plating" trap covered fully in Module 3.
Quick Check – Module 2

A WBS's "Platform" branch includes a work package for a mobile app, but nothing in the charter, scope statement, or requirements ever mentioned a mobile app. What does the 100% rule say about this?

Module 03 / 07

Validating & Controlling Scope

Two different failure modes threaten a well-defined scope after the WBS is built: scope creep (uncontrolled, unapproved additions) and gold-plating (the team's own well-meaning extras). Both erode the boundary Module 2 worked to set — from opposite directions.

What you'll learn
  • The difference between validating scope and quality control — they check different things
  • Scope creep vs. gold-plating, and why the second one feels harmless and isn't
  • How the scope baseline connects back to Part 3's change control process

Validating Scope ≠ Quality Control

These two are easy to confuse, but they check different things. Quality control (Part 3, Module 4) asks: is this deliverable correct — does it meet the specifications? Validating scope asks: does the customer or sponsor formally accept this deliverable as satisfying what was agreed? A deliverable can pass quality control (it's built exactly to spec) and still fail scope validation if the spec itself turns out to not be what the sponsor actually wanted — which is exactly why both checks exist, and why one doesn't substitute for the other.

Scope Creep vs. Gold-Plating

Who adds itHow it happensWhy it's a problem
Scope creepStakeholdersSmall, informal requests accepted one at a time, without going through change controlBudget and schedule silently no longer match the actual work being done
Gold-platingThe project teamAdding unrequested extras the team thinks would be niceSpends budget/time on something nobody asked for or agreed to pay for, however well-intentioned
Two paths a scope change can take
Same starting point, same-sized change — the only difference is whether it goes through the process from Part 3, Module 5.
Why gold-plating feels harmless: nobody's angry about it, nobody filed a complaint — the team just added something they thought was an improvement. But it still consumed budget and time that weren't allocated to it, and it still changed the deliverable without the sponsor's agreement. Good intentions don't exempt it from scope control.

Module 04 / 07

Network Diagrams

Work packages from the WBS become activities. Activities need an order. A network diagram is what turns "here's everything that needs doing" into "here's what depends on what" — the structure the critical path (Module 5) is calculated from.

What you'll learn
  • How to sequence activities using dependency relationships
  • The four dependency types, and why finish-to-start dominates in practice
  • How to read a simple network diagram

From Work Packages to Sequenced Activities

Each WBS work package (Module 2) typically breaks down further into one or more schedule activities. Sequencing asks: what has to finish before this can start? What can run in parallel? A network diagram (technically, the Precedence Diagramming Method) represents activities as nodes and dependencies as connecting arrows.

Four dependency types

TypeMeansExample
Finish-to-Start (FS)B can't start until A finishesCan't install fixtures until framing is done — by far the most common
Start-to-Start (SS)B can't start until A startsTesting can begin once development begins, run alongside it
Finish-to-Finish (FF)B can't finish until A finishesDocumentation can't be finalized until development is finalized
Start-to-Finish (SF)B can't finish until A startsRare — e.g. the old system can't be shut down until the new one starts
A simple network diagram — platform build
All finish-to-start here: Backend and Frontend can run in parallel once Requirements is signed off, but both must finish before Integration Testing starts.
Why this matters beyond the diagram: once activities are sequenced, you can calculate the shortest possible project duration and identify which activities have zero room to slip — that's the critical path, covered next.

Module 05 / 07

Critical Path & PERT

Two of the most exam-tested techniques in all of project management, and both genuinely useful in practice: finding the sequence of activities that actually determines your project's minimum duration, and estimating each activity's duration without pretending you know it for certain.

What you'll learn
  • What the critical path is, and why activities off it have "float"
  • The PERT three-point estimating formula, worked through a real example
  • Why the critical path can shift as a project proceeds

The Critical Path

The critical path is the longest sequence of dependent activities through the network diagram — and it directly sets the project's minimum possible duration. Any delay on a critical-path activity delays the whole project, day for day. Activities not on the critical path have float (or slack): room to slip without affecting the finish date.

Critical path highlighted, with float on the side branch
Critical path (solid, blue): Requirements → Backend → Integration → Launch = 25 days. Frontend Build could run up to 5 days late without moving the launch date.

PERT: Estimating Without Pretending Certainty

Three-point (PERT) estimating asks for three numbers per activity instead of one guess: an Optimistic (O) duration if everything goes right, a Most Likely (M) duration under normal conditions, and a Pessimistic (P) duration if things go wrong. The weighted formula favors the most-likely case while still accounting for the tails:

Expected Duration = (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6
6 days
Optimistic (O) — everything goes right
9 days
Most Likely (M) — normal conditions
18 days
Pessimistic (P) — things go wrong

Worked example, the platform's Integration Testing activity: (6 + 4×9 + 18) ÷ 6 = (6 + 36 + 18) ÷ 6 = 60 ÷ 6 = 10 days. Note the expected value (10) leans toward the most-likely estimate (9) but is pulled upward slightly by the wide pessimistic tail (18) — that's the formula doing its job.

It depends, and it moves: the critical path isn't fixed once at kickoff — if a non-critical activity (like Frontend Build above) runs long enough to eat all its float, it can become the new critical path. Recalculate after any significant schedule change, not just once.

Module 06 / 07

Monitoring the Schedule

A schedule is a prediction, and predictions drift. This module covers catching that drift on purpose (schedule variance, previewing Part 5's full Earned Value treatment) and the two standard levers for pulling a late schedule back — each with a different cost.

What you'll learn
  • What schedule variance measures, in plain terms (Part 5 covers the full formula)
  • Fast-tracking vs. crashing — what each does and what each costs
  • Why picking the wrong compression technique for the situation backfires

Schedule Variance, Briefly

Schedule variance compares how much work was actually planned to be done by now against how much has actually been accomplished — a negative variance means the project is behind. Part 5's Earned Value Management module covers the full calculation (SV = EV − PV) alongside its cost counterpart; here, the point is simpler: monitoring means checking this on a defined cadence, not discovering you're six weeks behind at the six-week mark.

Two Ways to Compress a Late Schedule

TechniqueWhat it doesPrimary costWhen it works
Fast-trackingRuns sequential activities in parallel insteadIncreased risk of rework if the parallel activities turn out to depend on each other after allWhen activities only had a soft, not a hard, dependency
CrashingAdds resources to shorten an activity's durationIncreased direct cost (more staff, overtime), with diminishing returnsWhen the activity is genuinely resource-constrained, not dependency-constrained
Fast-tracking vs. crashing, visually
Fast-tracking trades time for risk. Crashing trades time for money. Neither is free, and picking the wrong one for the situation makes things worse, not better.
The trap: fast-tracking two activities that turn out to have a hard dependency after all — say, running "finalize the accessibility retrofit design" in parallel with "order the materials" because it seemed independent, then discovering the design change means the wrong materials were ordered. The schedule looks compressed right up until the rework wipes out the gain and adds more delay than the technique saved.
