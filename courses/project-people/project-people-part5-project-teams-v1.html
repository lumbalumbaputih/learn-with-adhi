What Is a Team, Really?

A group (Part 4) becomes something more specific — a work team — the moment its combined output genuinely exceeds what the individuals could have produced separately. That's not a soft claim; it's the whole reason organisations reorganised around teams in the first place.

What you'll learn
  • The precise definition of a work team, and how it differs from a group
  • The four reasons teams became the default way of organising work

The Team Advantage

A work team is a group whose individual efforts result in a level of performance that is greater than the sum of the individual inputs. That "greater than the sum" clause is the entire point — a collection of skilled individuals who merely sit near each other is a group (Part 4); it becomes a team only once genuine synergy shows up in the output.

Why Teams Took Over

Teams became the default way organisations get work done for four concrete reasons: they are more flexible and responsive to changing events than traditional departments or other permanent groupings; they can quickly assemble, deploy, refocus and disband as the work demands; they facilitate employee participation in operating decisions; and they are an effective means for management to democratise organisations and increase employee motivation.

Group vs. team — the one-line test
Same three people, same individual skill — the difference is whether the whole genuinely exceeds the parts.

Module 02 / 05

Why Cross-Functional Teams Fail

Cross-functional teams promise the best of every discipline in one room. In practice, research by Behnam Tabrizi (2015) found most of them simply don't deliver — and the reasons are structural, not personal.

What you'll learn
  • The four structural reasons cross-functional teams fail
  • The four fixes that reliably address them

Why They Fail

  • Unclear governance and lack of accountability — nobody is actually on the hook for the team's overall outcome.
  • Goals lack specificity — the mandate is broad ("improve customer experience") rather than a measurable target.
  • Organisations fail to prioritise the team's success — members' real incentives still point back to their home department.
  • Teams operate in silos — engineers, designers, and salespeople each optimise for their own function rather than the shared output.

The Fix

  1. Every project requires an end-to-end accountable leader — one person whose job is the whole outcome, not just their functional slice.
  2. Every project needs established goals, resources, and deadlines — specificity closes the "goals lack specificity" gap directly.
  3. Teams must have the project's success as their main objective — not a secondary task layered on top of their "real" job.
  4. Every project should be constantly re-evaluated — silos and drift creep back in without a standing mechanism to catch them.
Four failure modes, four matching fixes
Tabrizi's four failure modes map one-to-one onto four concrete fixes — none of which require "better people," only better structure.
The trap to avoid: treating cross-functional failure as a personality or culture problem ("our engineers and salespeople just don't get along") when it's actually a structural one. Tabrizi's fixes are all structural — accountability, specificity, prioritisation, re-evaluation — because that's where the actual leverage sits.

Module 03 / 05

Building a High-Performing Project Team

Ericksen & Dyer's (2004) research is blunt about where high-performing project teams actually earn their advantage: not in the middle of the project, but in how they're mobilised at the very start.

What you'll learn
  • What separates high- and low-performing teams' mobilisation
  • Why the formative stage carries disproportionate weight

Two Very Different Starts

High-performing project teams mobilise much quicker and launch faster, through a comprehensive mobilisation strategy: content-clarification activities (elucidating the project's scope and requirements, gathering supportive background information, creating working documents), competency-based staffing, outreach activities, and participatory meetings.

Low-performing project teams, by contrast, run a limited mobilisation strategy: process-formation activities only (laying out plans and associated timetables, with little real content clarification), politically-based staffing, and programmed meetings where leaders tell and sell while members resist and question.

One fork in the road, at the formative stage
Both paths start from the identical moment — a kickoff. The split happens entirely in how that moment is handled, not in anything about the work itself.
The headline finding: actions taken in the formative stage are the single most important predictor of a project team's later performance. Whatever a PM does — or skips — in the first weeks of mobilisation has outsized, durable effects on everything that follows.

Module 04 / 05 · Synthesis

Applying It: Parts 1–4, in Practice

This module introduces no new source material — it's a practical synthesis, pulling together Parts 1 through 4 into one concrete question: when you're actually standing up a project team next month, what do you do differently because you now know all of this?

What you'll learn
  • How to apply attitudes, decision biases, motivation, and group theory while building a team
  • Four concrete actions, one per earlier part of this series

Four Lenses on the Same Team

Mobilising a project team (Module 3) is exactly the moment where everything from Parts 1–4 becomes practically useful at once. Here's how each part changes what you actually do:

🧠

From Part 1 — Screen for Fit, Not Just Skill

Weight observer-ratings over self-reports when assessing candidates; watch for Dark Triad red flags that look like confidence in an interview; check person-project fit, not just availability.

🎭

From Part 2 — Watch Your Own Biases

When evaluating who to staff, you're vulnerable to availability bias (favouring who comes to mind easily) and confirmation bias (favouring people who confirm your existing read of them). Use structured criteria, not gut feel alone.

🔥

From Part 3 — Match the Motivational Lever

McClelland's need for achievement wants challenging, well-defined goals; self-efficacy grows from early wins — so hand a new team a genuinely winnable first milestone, not the hardest problem first.

👥

From Part 4 — Expect the Stages, Shape the Contract

Storming is coming — plan for it rather than panicking. Set the psychological contract's terms explicitly and early. Watch early consensus for signs of groupthink rather than celebrating it as alignment.

One mobilisation decision, four checks
The same single decision — who joins the team — touches all four earlier parts at once.
The practical takeaway: Module 3 showed that formative-stage actions predict long-run team performance more than almost anything that follows. Parts 1–4 are what tell you specifically which formative-stage actions to take — not just "mobilise comprehensively," but mobilise comprehensively while screening for fit, checking your own biases, matching motivational levers, and shaping the group's early dynamics deliberately.
