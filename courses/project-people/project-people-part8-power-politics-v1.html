Module 01 / 05

What Is Power?

Power and political behaviour are natural processes in any group or organisation — not a sign something has gone wrong. Understanding how power actually works is what lets a project manager use it productively instead of being blindsided by it.

What you'll learn
  • The formal definition of power, and why it's a capacity, not an action
  • Dependency — the mechanism that actually generates power
  • How power differs from leadership, even though the two are closely related

Power, Defined

Power is the capacity of 'A' to influence B's behaviour so that B acts in accordance with A's wishes. Notice the word capacity: power is a potential, not an action. Someone can hold real power and never use it — the moment they stop being willing to use it, the power doesn't disappear, it just goes unexercised.

Where does that capacity come from? From dependency. The greater B's dependence on A, the greater A's power in that relationship. A can only have power over you if they control something you desire — money, information, approval, a resource, a decision. Remove the dependency and the power evaporates, no matter how senior A's title is.

Influence tactics are power translated into specific, observable moves — the concrete things A actually does to get B to comply. Political behaviour is the broader term for exercising power inside an organisational setting.

Dependency is what generates power — not title
A project sponsor who controls your budget, your scope sign-off, and your next assignment has power over you for exactly one reason: you depend on all three.

Power vs. Leadership

Power and leadership are closely intertwined — leaders use power to attain group goals — but they aren't the same thing, and the differences matter for how you diagnose a difficult working relationship.

DimensionLeadershipPower
Goal compatibilityRequires congruence between the leader's goals and those being ledDoesn't require goal compatibility — merely dependence
Direction of influenceFocuses on downward influence over followersEncompasses downward, lateral, AND upward influence
Research emphasisStyle — how supportive should a leader be? how much decision-making to share?Tactics — the specific moves used to gain compliance
Why this distinction matters: a junior analyst with deep system knowledge can hold real power over a senior manager who depends on that expertise (upward influence) without being anyone's "leader" in any formal sense. Power doesn't wait for a title or goal alignment — it just needs dependency.

Module 02 / 05

The Five Bases of Power

Where does power actually come from? French & Raven's classic model groups five distinct sources into two families — power that comes with the position, and power that comes with the person.

What you'll learn
  • The five bases of power — coercive, reward, legitimate, expert, referent
  • Why formal power and personal power behave so differently
  • How power actually plays out on a project, per Walker & Newcombe's research

Formal vs. Personal Power

Formal power is based on an individual's position in an organisation, and comes from the ability to coerce or reward, or from formal authority. Personal power comes from an individual's unique characteristics — their expertise, or the respect others simply give them.

The five bases of power, in two families
Formal power disappears the day the title does. Personal power walks out the door with the person — which is exactly why it tends to be the more durable kind.

Power on an Actual Project

Walker & Newcombe's research applying this model to construction projects found a distinctive pattern. The contract is the source of legitimate power on a project — but that legitimate power is frequently undermined by the sheer need to bargain (architects and contractors negotiate constantly, which erodes a pure chain-of-command view of authority). Meanwhile, reward and coercion can actually be applied more easily by people outside the formal authority structure than inside it.

Expert power is a major force on projects specifically because knowledge is scarce and valuable — the specialist who understands the one tricky subsystem has real leverage regardless of their position in the org chart. Referent power, based on a track record of substantial achievement, is reinforced by charisma (which you'll meet properly in Part 7).

The project-specific trap: a PM who leans only on legitimate power ("because the contract says so") discovers it is the weakest base of the five on a real project — precisely because so much of project life runs on bargaining, not command. Expert and referent power tend to do the actual work of getting things done.

Module 03 / 05

Responses to Influence & the Stakeholder Grid

Exercising power produces one of three reactions — and mapping stakeholders by how much they care and how much sway they have is the practical tool that turns "managing stakeholders" from a vague aspiration into an actual plan.

What you'll learn
  • The three responses people give to an influence attempt
  • How to build and read a Stakeholder Interest & Influence Grid
  • Why "migrating" a stakeholder between quadrants is itself a deliberate strategy

Three Responses to Influence

Falbe & Yukl's research found people respond to an influence attempt in one of three ways, and which one shows up is entirely contextual:

🚫

Opposition

Active resistance — the target pushes back, argues, or refuses outright.

✅

Compliance

The target does what's asked, but without any real buy-in — minimum viable cooperation.

🤝

Commitment

The target genuinely agrees and invests real effort — the response every influence attempt is actually aiming for.

The Stakeholder Interest & Influence Grid

Mapping stakeholders is the key to managing them, and to building an effective communications plan. Every stakeholder can be placed on two axes: how much interest they have in the project's outcome, and how much influence (power) they hold over it.

The Stakeholder Interest & Influence Grid
The quadrant isn't a life sentence — the priority is to identify where each stakeholder currently sits, and where useful, deliberately work to migrate them toward a more beneficial one.
Migration in practice: a regulator who currently sits in "Monitor" (low interest, low power over day-to-day decisions, but real power if a breach occurs) is worth deliberately moving toward "Keep Informed" with proactive updates — cheap insurance against a future escalation that would instantly spike their influence.

Module 04 / 05

Organisational Politics: Necessary Evil?

"Politics" has a bad reputation, but most managers who actually practise it describe it as unavoidable — a necessary evil, not an optional vice. The real question isn't whether to engage in it, but whether it's done ethically.

What you'll learn
  • Concrete examples of political behaviour in organisations
  • Why scarce resources and ambiguous outcomes make politics close to unavoidable
  • Where the ethical line actually sits

What Political Behaviour Looks Like

🤐

Withholding information

Keeping key facts back to preserve an advantage.

🤝

Joining a coalition

Aligning with others to gain collective leverage.

📢

Whistle-blowing

Exposing wrongdoing — political in the sense of reshaping power, not necessarily wrong.

🗣️

Spreading rumours

Shaping perception informally, outside any official channel.

📰

Leaking to the media

Taking an internal matter outside the organisation to force a response.

🔄

Exchanging favours

Trading mutual benefit — the oldest form of organisational currency.

📣

Lobbying

Actively campaigning for or against a decision or a person.

Why Politics Is (Almost) Unavoidable

Organisations are made up of individuals and groups with genuinely different values, goals, and interests — and they're competing for limited resources: budgets, space, project responsibilities. On top of that, many outcomes are open to interpretation. What even counts as "project success"? Under conditions of ambiguity and scarcity, political behaviour isn't a character flaw showing up — it's close to structurally guaranteed.

Three structural pressures that make politics close to unavoidable
Remove any one of the three pressures and politics would recede — but on a real project, all three are almost always present at once.
✓
Political behaviour can be ethical and necessary — as long as it doesn't directly harm anyone else
The module's own ethical line
⚠️
Most managers who report using political behaviour call it a "necessary evil"
Self-reported practitioner view
❓
Most managers also report never being formally trained to use political behaviour effectively
A practical skills gap
The ethical test that actually matters: the module's own line is not "avoid politics entirely" (naive — ambiguity and scarcity make that close to impossible) but "don't directly harm anyone else." Building a coalition to advocate for your project's priority is political and fine. Leaking confidential information purely to sabotage a colleague crosses the line the module draws.

Power Applied to Project Management
Walker, Anthony, and Robert Newcombe. "The Positive Use of Power on a Major Construction Project." Construction Management and Economics 18, no. 1 (2000): 37–44.
The construction-project study cited directly in the source lecture, establishing that contracts are the source of legitimate power on a project, and that this power is routinely undermined by the need to bargain between parties such as architects and contractors.
