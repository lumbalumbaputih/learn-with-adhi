Defining Change & the Forces Behind It

Change is simply making things different. Planned change is the intentional, goal-oriented version — and five external forces are what keep making it necessary, whether an organisation wants it or not.

What you'll learn
  • Planned change, and what a change agent actually does
  • The five forces that drive organisations toward change
  • Why change agents focus on people and their interactions, not just processes

Planned Change & Change Agents

Planned change consists of intentional, goal-oriented change activities. It improves an organisation's ability to adapt to changes in its environment, and it helps change employee behaviour specifically. A change agent is a person — from inside or outside the organisation — who acts as a catalyst and assumes responsibility for managing change activities. Their focus is squarely on the people in the organisation and their interactions, not just the technical mechanics of the change itself.

Five Forces for Change

Five external forces, one organisation under pressure to adapt
None of these forces are under the organisation's control — only its response to them is.
Project scenario: a project launched 18 months ago to deploy a legacy ERP system is now competing for budget against a cloud-native alternative because the technology force shifted mid-project. A change agent's job here isn't to relitigate the original decision — it's to manage how the people on the team process and adapt to that shift.

Module 02 / 07

Resistance to Change

Resistance isn't a character flaw to stamp out — it's a natural, arguably positive signal (better than silent apathy). But some forms are far easier to manage than others.

What you'll learn
  • Why resistance is natural, and sometimes a healthy sign
  • The four forms resistance takes, and why deferred resistance is the hardest
  • The individual and organisational sources of resistance
  • Eight standard strategies for overcoming resistance

Forms of Resistance

Resistance to change can be overt (voiced complaints) or implicit (quiet withdrawal), and immediate or deferred. Overt, immediate resistance is the easiest to deal with — it's visible and attributable. Implicit, deferred resistance — showing up later as loss of loyalty and motivation, increased errors, or rising absenteeism — is much harder, because the delay clouds the link between the original source and the eventual reaction.

Four forms of resistance, by how hard they are to manage
The further a reaction drifts from "overt and immediate," the harder it becomes to connect the resistance back to its true cause.

Why People Resist — and How to Help Them Stop

Common reasons people resist, beyond the structural overt/implicit split: inertia (comfort with the status quo), indecision (more options feel like more freedom, so committing to one feels like a loss), fear of making mistakes, a lack of role models or best practices to copy, and simply not liking the new thing regardless of how sensible it is. Sources split into individual (perception, personality, needs, selective information processing) and organisational (structural make-up, group inertia).

💬

Education & Communication

Explain the logic of the change before it lands, not after.

🙋

Participation & Involvement

Let affected people help design the change, not just receive it.

🛟

Build Support & Commitment

Give people room to process the emotional cost of the change.

🤝

Positive Relationships

Trust in the messenger buys tolerance for the message.

⚖️

Implement Fairly

Perceived fairness of process matters as much as the outcome.

🎯

Select for Change

Where possible, staff the change with people who adapt well.

⚠️

Coercion

Fast but corrosive — a genuine last resort, not a first move.

🎭

Manipulation/Co-optation

Ethically risky; erodes trust if discovered, so use with real caution.

Reframe: resistance is a natural and arguably positive state — it beats silence and apathy, because it tells you someone still cares enough about the outcome to push back. A project with zero resistance to a major change is more likely disengaged than aligned.

Module 03 / 07

Change Models: Lewin's Three Steps & Kotter's Eight

Lewin's 1951 model is the foundation almost every later change framework builds on — including Kotter's eight-step plan, which maps cleanly onto Lewin's three phases.

What you'll learn
  • Lewin's unfreezing → movement → refreezing model
  • Driving forces vs. restraining forces, and the status-quo bias
  • Kotter's eight steps, and exactly how they map onto Lewin's three

Lewin's Three-Step Model

Unfreezing overcomes the pressures of both individual resistance and group conformity. Movement is the transition itself. Refreezing stabilises the change by balancing driving forces (which direct behaviour away from the status quo) against restraining forces (which hinder movement away from the existing equilibrium). The status quo itself is a cognitive bias — an irrational preference for the current state of affairs simply because it's current.

Kotter's Eight Steps, Mapped Onto Lewin's Three

Kotter's 8-step plan, explicitly built on Lewin's model
Kotter's steps 1–2 unfreeze, 3–6 drive the movement, and 7–8 refreeze — the same three-phase shape Lewin described in 1951, made more actionable.

A common mistake: organisations frequently skip straight to "movement" — announcing and launching the change — without doing any real unfreezing first. Without loosening individual resistance and group conformity beforehand, the change meets maximum resistance at exactly the point it's most fragile.

Module 04 / 07

Building a Change-Ready Culture

Instead of only helping an organisation adapt to change when it arrives, you can build a culture that embraces change as a standing capability — through innovation and continuous organisational learning.

What you'll learn
  • What drives innovation, and why it always needs an idea champion
  • What a learning organisation is, and its five characteristics

Innovation & the Idea Champion

Innovation is a new idea applied to initiating or improving a product, process, or service. It tends to flow from four sources: organic (flexible) structural variables, long-tenured management, slack resources, and strong inter-unit communication. But none of that matters without an idea champion — an individual who actively promotes the innovation, pushing it past the organisational inertia that would otherwise quietly kill it.

Sources of innovation converge on one person who carries it forward
The four sources create the conditions; the idea champion is what actually converts those conditions into an adopted innovation.

Learning Organisations

A learning organisation has developed a continuous capacity to adapt and change. Its characteristics: it holds a shared vision, discards old ways of thinking, views the organisation as a system of relationships (not isolated silos), communicates openly, and works together toward that shared vision.

🎯

Shared Vision

Everyone is pulling toward the same destination, not just their own department's goals.

🗑️

Discards Old Thinking

Willing to abandon mental models that no longer fit reality.

🕸️

System of Relationships

Sees the organisation as interconnected, not a set of independent boxes.

📣

Open Communication

Information moves freely rather than getting hoarded or filtered.

🤲

Works Together

Collective effort toward the shared vision, not isolated heroics.

Project application: a project that operates like a mini learning organisation — openly communicating lessons learned mid-project rather than saving them for a post-mortem nobody reads — adapts to the five forces for change (Module 1) in real time, instead of being blindsided by them at the next milestone review.

Module 05 / 07

Work Stress

Stress is a dynamic condition where someone faces an opportunity, constraint, or demand related to something they desire — with an outcome that's perceived as both uncertain and important. Not all stress is equal, and project managers face a specific, well-documented version of it.

What you'll learn
  • Challenge stressors vs. hindrance stressors — and why one does more damage
  • The three sources of stress: environmental, organisational, personal
  • Why project work is a specifically stress-prone structure (Pinto, Dawood & Pinto 2014)

Challenge Stressors vs. Hindrance Stressors

TypeWhat it isRelative harm
Challenge stressorsWorkload, pressure to complete tasks, time urgencyReal stress, but can be motivating
Hindrance stressorsThings that keep you from reaching your goals — e.g. red tape, uncooperative colleaguesGreater harm than challenge stressors
Same stress category, different harm levels
Both are "stress," but hindrance stressors — the ones that actively block you — cause measurably greater harm than challenge stressors.

Sources of Stress

🌍

Environmental

Economic, political, and technological uncertainty outside anyone's direct control.

🏢

Organisational

Task demands, role demands, and interpersonal demands from the job itself.

🏠

Personal

Family relationships, financial pressure beyond earning capacity, personality-driven disposition.

Why project managers specifically are at risk (Pinto, Dawood & Pinto 2014): projects are typically fast-paced and dynamic, requiring constant alignment with organisational strategy, all while simultaneously balancing competing concerns — schedule, budget, stakeholder satisfaction, and quality. That combination is structurally stress-prone in a way that steady-state operational roles usually are not.

Module 06 / 07

Burnout & Managing Stress in Projects

Unmanaged stress doesn't plateau — it compounds, following a predictable progression toward burnout. The good news: specific, documented interventions genuinely change the outcome.

What you'll learn
  • The physiological, psychological, and behavioural consequences of stress
  • Burnout's definition and its six-stage progression
  • Individual, organisational, and project-specific ways to manage it

Consequences of Stress (They're Additive)

💓

Physiological

Blood pressure, headaches, stroke risk.

😣

Psychological

Dissatisfaction, tension, anxiety, irritability, boredom, procrastination — worst when roles are unclear amid conflicting demands.

🚬

Behavioural

Changed job behaviours, more smoking/drinking, altered eating, rapid speech, fidgeting, sleep disorders.

Burnout

Maslach & Leiter (2008) define burnout as a psychological syndrome of emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and reduced personal accomplishment. It follows a recognisable progression:

The six-stage progression toward total burnout
By "total burnout," the person is physically, mentally, emotionally, and spiritually drained — and feels they have nothing left to offer.

The counter-intuitive finding: in the Pinto, Dawood & Pinto (2014) project-team study, only co-worker support meaningfully reduced burnout onset — supervisor support did not show the same protective effect. If you're designing a support intervention for a stressed project team, peer-to-peer mechanisms may matter more than another layer of top-down check-ins.
