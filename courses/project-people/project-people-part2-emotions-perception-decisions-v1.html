Module 01 / 06

Emotions, Moods & Affect

Affect is the umbrella term covering the whole range of feelings people experience at work — and it splits into two things that get casually conflated but behave very differently: emotions and moods.

What you'll learn
  • The difference between affect, emotions, and moods
  • Why emotions are specific and action-oriented, while moods are general and longer-lasting
  • The main sources of emotions and moods at work — including some surprising ones

Three Related Terms

Emotions are intense feelings directed at someone or something. Moods are feelings that are less intense than emotions and lack a contextual stimulus — they can simply arise without a specific triggering event. Affect is the broad umbrella term covering both.

How Emotions Differ From Moods

  • Emotions are more specific, more numerous, and more intense in nature (e.g. anger, grief, joy).
  • Moods are more general and longer-term, and are usually classified simply as positive or negative.
  • Emotions are action-oriented — nearly every decision we make has some emotional component behind it.
  • Emotions feed moods: a sharp emotional spike can colour your general mood for the rest of the day.
Emotions vs. moods, on two dimensions
Emotions are the spike; moods are the weather that spike leaves behind.

Sources of Emotions and Moods

Where do emotions and moods actually come from, day to day? The sources are broader than people expect:

  • Personality — affects intensity: how strongly an individual experiences their emotions.
  • Time of day and day of the week — produce patterns of positive and negative affect.
  • Weather — e.g. the hypothesised link between low Vitamin D and a depressive state.
  • Stress — the ongoing management of pressure shapes affect over time.
  • Social activities, sleep, age, and gender — sleep deprivation in particular is a significant, underappreciated driver.
  • Events — lower in the order of influence than the factors above, but still a real source of mood change. Even just remembering an event can evoke a shiver of excitement or the heat of anger, nearly as powerfully as living it.

Common misattribution
The counter-intuitive bit: when a team's mood sours, the instinct is to look for "the event" that caused it. But events are the weakest of the six sources listed here — sleep deprivation, stress load, and basic personality differences usually explain more of a team's day-to-day affect than any single incident does. A PM chasing "what happened?" may be looking in the wrong place entirely.

Module 02 / 06

Emotional Labour & Emotional Intelligence

Project managers aren't usually thought of as doing "emotional labour" the way a waiter or a call-centre agent is — but anyone managing expectations from a team, a sponsor, and a customer at once is doing more of it than they realise.

What you'll learn
  • What emotional labour is, and the difference between surface and deep acting
  • Why emotional labour matters specifically for PMs, not just service staff
  • Emotional intelligence, defined — and the genuine debate over whether it's a real, measurable thing

Emotional Labour

Emotional labour involves managing and displaying emotions consistent with an organisation's display rules — regardless of whether those emotions match what the person is actually feeling. Surface acting means faking an outward expression ("wearing a mask"); deep acting means consciously changing how you actually feel, then expressing that genuinely-felt emotion.

Project managers are not primarily in service roles, but they interact constantly with team members, sponsors, and customers — all of whom carry emotional expectations (confident, positive, in-control) regardless of what a PM actually feels under deadline pressure. That makes substantial emotional labour a routine, if unnamed, part of the job.

The cost: over time, emotional labour — especially the surface-acting kind — leads to burnout, emotional exhaustion, work disengagement, higher turnover, and increased stress. The "always confident, always positive" PM persona is not free; it's a sustained cognitive effort that eventually needs to be paid down.

Emotional Intelligence

Emotional intelligence (EI) is a person's ability to (1) perceive emotions in themselves and others, (2) understand the meaning of those emotions, and (3) regulate their own emotions accordingly.

EI: the case for, and the case against
The debate is genuinely unresolved — treat EI as a useful lens, not a settled, precisely measured trait.

Module 03 / 06

Perception & Attribution Theory

People's behaviour is based on their perception of what reality is — not on reality itself. That single sentence explains more project conflict than almost any other idea in this series.

What you'll learn
  • Why perception, not objective reality, is what drives behaviour
  • Attribution theory's three factors for judging internal vs. external causes
  • How to apply all three factors to a single real scenario

What Perception Is

Perception is the process by which individuals organise and interpret their sensory impressions to give meaning to their environment. What people perceive can differ substantially from objective reality — which is precisely why perception matters so much in organisational behaviour: people's behaviour is based on their perception of what reality is, not on reality itself. The world as it is perceived is the world that is behaviourally important.

Attribution Theory

Attribution theory explains how we judge people's behaviour differently depending on the meaning we attach to it. When we observe someone's behaviour, we try to determine whether it was internally caused (under the person's own control) or externally caused (the situation forced it). That judgment rests on three factors:

The three factors, applied to a late-arriving contractor
All three factors must be weighed together — a single data point rarely settles internal vs. external on its own.
FactorQuestionHigh value points to…
DistinctivenessDoes this person behave differently in different situations?External attribution (it's unusual for them)
ConsensusWould most people respond the same way in this situation?External attribution (the situation, not the person)
ConsistencyDoes this person respond the same way over time?Internal attribution (it's a pattern, not a one-off)
Internal vs. external, defined: an internally caused behaviour is one we believe is under the person's own control (e.g. "he was late because he stayed out until 3am"). An externally caused behaviour is one we believe the situation forced (e.g. "he was late because of a car accident on his route"). Attribution theory is entirely about how we decide which story to believe.

Module 04 / 06

Decision Making & Bounded Rationality

Nobody makes decisions with perfect information or unlimited mental processing power — and pretending otherwise is itself a decision-making mistake.

What you'll learn
  • How perception feeds into the problem-identification and decision chain
  • Bounded rationality — why we simplify problems rather than fully solve them
  • Satisficing vs. optimising, and why "good enough" usually wins

Perceptions and Individual Decision Making

Decision making starts with the decision maker's own perception. A problem is a perceived discrepancy between the current state of affairs and a desired state — it has to be noticed and judged as a problem before anything else happens. From there, decisions are choices made among alternatives, developed from whatever data is perceived (not necessarily actual) as relevant.

The perception-to-decision chain
Every step in the chain runs on perceived information — change what's perceived at any step, and the final decision changes too, even if objective reality hasn't moved.

Bounded Rationality

Because the human mind can't formulate and fully solve genuinely complex problems, people operate under bounded rationality: constructing simplified models that extract the essential features from a problem without capturing all its complexity, then behaving rationally within the limits of that simplified model.

In practice: once a problem is identified, people search for a limited, easy-to-find, highly visible list of criteria and alternatives — not an exhaustive one. They review alternatives that differ only slightly from the current choice, following familiar, well-worn paths, until they land on one that's "good enough." That's called satisficing — the first acceptable solution, not the optimal one.

Project application: a procurement decision that picks the first vendor meeting a "good enough" bar (rather than exhaustively comparing every possible vendor) isn't a failure of diligence — it's bounded rationality working exactly as the theory predicts. Recognising this helps a PM set a deliberately higher "good enough" bar for high-stakes decisions, rather than assuming people will naturally search harder just because the stakes are higher.

Module 05 / 06

Biases & Errors in Decision Making

Bounded rationality explains why we simplify. These four biases explain the specific, predictable ways that simplification goes wrong.

What you'll learn
  • Hindsight, anchoring, confirmation, and availability bias — defined precisely
  • Real-world examples of each, including credit-rating agencies and performance appraisals
  • How to recognise each bias operating in your own project decisions

The Four Biases

🔙

Hindsight Bias

The human tendency to be unable to reconstruct past states of knowledge or belief that have since changed — "I knew it all along," even when you didn't.

⚓

Anchoring Bias

A tendency to fixate on the first piece of information received, then fail to adequately adjust for information that comes after.

🔍

Confirmation Bias

Seeking out information that reaffirms past choices, while discounting information that contradicts past judgments.

💭

Availability Bias

Basing judgments on whatever information is most readily available in memory — which is not the same as whatever is most statistically likely.

Confirmation Bias in Practice

The rational decision-making model assumes we objectively gather information. We don't — we selectively gather it. We seek out sources most likely to tell us what we already want to hear, give too much weight to supporting information, and too little to contradicting information, even when we're being critical and sceptical of the exact same type of evidence when it challenges a view we already hold.

Availability Bias in Practice

Events that are emotionally vivid or have happened recently are more available in memory — which leads people to overestimate how likely similarly dramatic (but actually rare) events are. This is precisely why managers doing performance appraisals tend to weight an employee's recent behaviour far more heavily than behaviour from six or nine months earlier, and why credit-rating agencies can issue overly positive ratings by relying too heavily on information that debt issuers — who have every incentive to present favourable data — choose to supply.

Anchoring bias: the first number sets the frame
Subsequent estimates move away from the anchor, but rarely far enough — the first number keeps exerting a pull long after new information arrives.
