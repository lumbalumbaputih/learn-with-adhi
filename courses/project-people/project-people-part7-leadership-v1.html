Module 01 / 06

Leadership vs. Management

Every project needs both — but they are not the same skill, and confusing them is how competent managers fail to actually lead. Day & Antonakis (2012) draw the line precisely.

What you'll learn
  • Day & Antonakis's definition of leadership vs. management
  • The full manager-vs-leader contrast, trait by trait
  • Why "successful leadership requires good management" — but doesn't stop there

Two Different Activities

Leadership is a purpose-driven activity grounded in values, ideals, vision, symbols, and emotional exchanges. Management is a task-driven activity grounded in rationality, bureaucratic means, and the fulfilment of contractual obligations. A project manager's functional role genuinely overlaps both — but they are distinct skill sets, and a PM strong in one is not automatically strong in the other.

Day & Antonakis's own conclusion is carefully balanced: successful leadership requires good management — but leadership goes beyond management. You cannot lead a chaotic, unmanaged project into success through vision alone; but you also cannot manage your way into extraordinary, above-expectation performance through process alone.

Module 02 / 06

Early Theories: Traits & Behaviours

Leadership research began by asking "who are leaders?" (trait theories), then shifted to "what do leaders do?" (behavioural theories). Both left an important gap that the next module's contingency theories had to fill.

What you'll learn
  • Trait theories: which personal qualities actually predict leadership (and what they predict better)
  • Behavioural theories: task-, people-, and change-oriented behaviour
  • Why both approaches were eventually judged incomplete

Trait Theories

Trait theories assume leaders have certain personal qualities and characteristics that differentiate them from non-leaders. Robust research (Judge et al. 2002) finds the Big Five personality traits predict leadership emergence — especially extraversion, the single strongest predictor. Self-monitoring, emotional intelligence, and self-efficacy are also related. More surprisingly, even the socially undesirable traits of narcissism and social dominance predict leadership emergence — narcissistic leaders often favour bold, aggressive, magnanimous actions specifically to draw attention to their vision.

The critical limitation: traits do a noticeably better job predicting leader emergence (who gets seen as a leader and rises) than leader effectiveness (who actually performs well once there). Trait theory also largely assumes leadership sits entirely within the person, ignoring the surrounding context — a serious gap, since the same traits can produce wildly different outcomes in different situations.

Behavioural Theories

Behavioural theories shift the question from "who leaders are" to "what leaders do." Research identified two primary dimensions, later joined by a third:

  • Task-oriented behaviour — goal-centred; letting followers know what's expected of them, using uniform procedures, maintaining clear performance standards.
  • People-oriented behaviour — relationship-centred; acting friendly and approachable, showing concern for others' welfare.
  • Change-oriented behaviour (Yukl, 1998) — a third dimension involving creativity, risk-taking, and a focus on the future.
Two axes, plus a third dimension
Task and people orientation were the original two dimensions; Yukl (1998) added change-orientation as a third, cutting across both.
Why behavioural theory also fell short: research following this approach produced contradictory findings and failed to identify consistent relationships between a leader's behaviour and their team's performance. Knowing someone is high in "task orientation," for instance, didn't reliably predict whether their team actually performed better — a strong hint that context, not just behaviour in the abstract, was the missing variable. That hint is exactly what the next module's contingency theories set out to address.

Contingency Theories: Fiedler & LMX

Contingency theory's core assumption: context changes everything. There should be an appropriate match between a leader's style and the demands of the situation — and once researchers started taking followers seriously too, they discovered leaders don't treat every follower the same way, either.

What you'll learn
  • The Fiedler Contingency Model: the 3 factors that determine situational favourableness
  • Leader-Member Exchange (LMX): in-groups, out-groups, and what high-quality LMX actually produces

The Fiedler Contingency Model (1964)

Because Fiedler treats leadership style as a relatively fixed personality trait, his model's practical advice is unusual: don't try to change the leader — change which situations you put them in.

Leader-Member Exchange (LMX)

LMX theory observes that leaders don't form one uniform relationship with "the team" — they form individual relationships with each member, and those relationships vary sharply in quality, based on interpersonal similarity (demographic, attitude/personality, or competence).

"In-group" (high-quality LMX)"Out-group" (low-quality LMX)

The uncomfortable implication: LMX quality is based partly on interpersonal similarity — meaning a PM can unintentionally build a stronger relationship with team members who simply remind them of themselves, while a genuinely high-performing but "different" team member drifts into the out-group through no fault of their own. Deliberately checking who's in your own in-group, and why, is one of the few concrete ways a PM can counteract this.

Module 04 / 06

Charismatic & Transformational Leadership

Charisma is an attribution followers make, not a fixed trait a leader either has or lacks. And not all charismatic leaders are equal — the "socialised" kind, and the broader transformational leadership model, consistently produce the strongest outcomes of any approach in this module.

What you'll learn
  • Conger & Kanungo's charismatic-leadership behaviours, and why "good vs. bad" charisma matters
  • Socialised charismatic leaders, specifically
  • The Full Range of Leadership Model, from laissez-faire to the four transformational behaviours

Charismatic Leadership

Conger & Kanungo (1998) describe charisma as an attribution: followers assign heroic or extraordinary leadership ability to someone when they observe specific behaviours — willingness to take personal risk and dissatisfaction with the status quo, a compellingly articulated vision, unconventional behaviours in pursuit of desired change, and genuine sensitivity to follower needs. Critically, charisma isn't a fixed personal quality the leader simply "has" — it's something followers confer based on what they see.

Why the evaluation matters: charisma theory is critiqued for fixating on individual leader characteristics without distinguishing good charismatic leaders (who serve the organisation and its people) from bad ones (who serve themselves). The same observable behaviours — risk-taking, an unconventional compelling vision — can belong to either, which is exactly why Module 1's Dark Triad content (narcissism in particular) matters here too.

Socialised Charismatic Leaders

A socialised charismatic leader has an egalitarian, self-transcendent, empowering personality. They pursue organisation-driven goals (not personal ones) and promote feelings of empowerment, personal growth, and equal participation in their followers — using rewards specifically to reinforce behaviour consistent with the organisation's own vision and mission. Where a self-serving charismatic leader demands admiration, a socialised one is driven to serve others.

The Full Range of Leadership Model

This model lays out a spectrum of leadership styles by effectiveness, from least to most. Transactional leadership relies on social-exchange obligations — guiding followers by clarifying roles and tasks. Transformational leadership inspires followers to transcend their own self-interest, producing a profound effect on their performance.

The Full Range of Leadership Model, by effectiveness
Laissez-faire is the most passive and least effective style. Management-by-exception only shows up when there's already a problem — often too late. Contingent reward works, but won't produce above-and-beyond effort. Only the four transformational behaviours reliably do.

Module 05 / 06

Shared Leadership

What if leadership didn't belong to one person at all? Hoegl & Muethel's 2016 field study of 96 virtual software-development project teams found shared leadership works — under three specific conditions, and against three predictable forms of resistance from the people who'd have to let go of control.

What you'll learn
  • What shared leadership actually is, and the 3 conditions it requires
  • Why formal leaders resist it — and the 5 strategies that enable it anyway

Leadership, Broadly Distributed

Shared leadership assumes leadership is broadly distributed rather than assigned to one person — people within the team and organisation lead each other, as the moment and the task require. It's achievable only if three conditions hold at once: formal leaders are willing to delegate power, the group has a genuinely collaborative culture of support, and team members have the actual ability to influence others through persuasion (not just formal authority).

Traditional (vertical) vs. shared leadership
Vertical leadership routes all direction through one node. Shared leadership routes it through whoever the moment and the task call for.

Why Leaders Resist It — and What Enables It Anyway

🏛️

Traditional Understanding

Leaders often hold a conventional, single-person model of what leadership is supposed to look like.

😤

Overconfidence

Leaders can be overconfident in their own singular role and judgment relative to the team's.

😟

Fear of Dispensability

Sharing leadership can feel like making yourself replaceable — a real fear, even when unfounded.

Hoegl & Muethel's strategies for enabling shared leadership anyway: accept the new rules of the game, respect team members' competencies, actively encourage leadership behaviours in others, loosen the reins and avoid the "responsibility trap" (believing only you can be accountable), and genuinely become a true team member rather than only its head.

Project scenario: on a cross-functional project, the engineer naturally takes the lead during a technical design debate, the client-relationship specialist takes the lead during a tense stakeholder negotiation, and a third member takes the lead organising the final handover. No single person is "the leader" throughout — leadership moved to wherever the task's demands matched someone's actual competence, exactly as shared leadership predicts.
