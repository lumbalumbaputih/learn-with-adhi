Attitudes: Components & Cognitive Dissonance

An attitude is an evaluative statement or judgment — positive or negative — about an object, a person, or an event. Before you can read a team, predict a stakeholder's reaction, or understand why a perfectly rational contractor is suddenly digging in, you need the three-part anatomy underneath every attitude.

What you'll learn
  • The three components of an attitude: cognitive, affective, behavioural
  • What cognitive dissonance is, and the three ways people resolve it
  • When attitudes actually predict behaviour — and when they don't

The Three Components of an Attitude

An attitude isn't one thing — it's three, working together. The cognitive component is the belief: an opinion or intellectual understanding of the way things are. The affective component is the emotional or "feelings" part — and it's the critical piece, because it's what actually drives behavioural outcomes. The behavioural component is the intention to act in a certain way toward someone or something.

Notice the word intention. An intention to behave is not the same as the behaviour itself — the behaviour may or may not follow, and it's shaped by everything else going on around the attitude.

Same situation, three layers. The affective layer is the one that actually converts a belief into pressure to act.

Cognitive Dissonance

Cognitive dissonance is any incompatibility between two or more attitudes, or between an attitude and a behaviour. It produces genuine psychological discomfort — and because that discomfort is unpleasant, people are motivated to reduce it and restore consistency.

There are exactly three ways to resolve it: change the attitude itself, modify the behaviour to match the attitude, or rationalise the gap (find a justification that makes the inconsistency feel acceptable). Most people reach for rationalisation first — it's the lowest-effort fix, even though it doesn't actually close the gap.

🔄

Change the Attitude

The highest-effort, most durable fix: genuinely update the belief or feeling so it matches the behaviour.

🛠️

Modify the Behaviour

Stop doing the thing that conflicts with the attitude — bring the action in line with the belief instead.

💬

Rationalise

Construct a justification that makes the inconsistency feel okay, without actually changing either side.

Project scenario: a project sponsor who publicly champions "data-driven decisions" keeps overruling the dashboard with gut calls. That's dissonance between stated attitude and behaviour. Watch for which resolution path they take — do they start trusting the data (attitude change), stop overruling it (behaviour change), or start explaining why "this particular case is different" every time (rationalisation)? The third pattern, repeated often enough, is a reliable tell that the stated attitude was never really load-bearing.

Predicting Behaviour From Attitudes

Predicting behaviour from attitudes is more art than science, but a few relationships hold up reasonably well:

  • Specificity matches specificity. Specific attitudes predict specific behaviours; general attitudes only predict general patterns of behaviour.
  • Values strengthen the link. The more tightly an attitude connects to a value someone genuinely holds dear, the stronger — and more predictive — the relationship to behaviour.
  • Social pressure weakens it. High social pressure can suppress the attitude-behaviour link, producing disagreement between what someone believes and what they visibly do.
  • Experience strengthens it. Attitudes formed from direct personal experience are stronger predictors of future behaviour than attitudes picked up secondhand.

Module 02 / 06

Job Attitudes

People hold thousands of attitudes, but the study of organisational behaviour narrows in on a small set of work-related ones. Four matter most for a project environment: job satisfaction, job involvement, psychological empowerment, and organisational commitment.

What you'll learn
  • What job satisfaction and job involvement actually measure
  • Psychological empowerment, and the construction-industry evidence behind it
  • Organisational commitment, and why it predicts more than just "staying"

Job Satisfaction & Job Involvement

Job satisfaction is a positive feeling about a job, resulting from an evaluation of its characteristics. A person high in job satisfaction holds positive feelings about their job; a person low in it holds negative feelings. Job involvement is related but distinct — it measures the degree to which people identify psychologically with their job, and how much they consider their perceived performance level important to their own self-worth. Someone with high job involvement doesn't just like the job; they treat doing it well as part of who they are.

Psychological Empowerment

A close relative of job involvement is psychological empowerment: the degree to which employees' beliefs influence their work environment, their sense of competence, the meaningfulness of their job, and their perceived autonomy. It's not a perk management hands out — it's how empowered an employee genuinely feels, which is a different thing from how much formal authority they've been given.

The nuance that matters: one study of nursing managers in Singapore found good leaders empower staff by involving them in decisions and giving discretion to "do their own thing" — but another study found that, for teachers in India, self-perceived competence didn't translate into more innovative behaviour. The lesson: empowerment initiatives need to be tailored to the culture and the behavioural outcome you actually want, not applied as a one-size-fits-all leadership recipe.

Organisational Commitment

Commitment is the degree to which an individual identifies with a particular entity — here, a project — and its goals, and wishes to maintain membership in it. It is one of the highest-leverage attitudes a project manager can cultivate, because of what it reliably produces downstream.

Personality & the Big Five

"Personality" doesn't mean charm or a winning smile — psychologists use it to describe the dynamic growth and development of a person's whole psychological system, captured through measurable traits. One model, the Big Five, has the strongest research base of any personality framework in organisational behaviour.

What you'll learn
  • How personality is actually measured — self-report vs. observer-rating surveys
  • The five dimensions of the Big Five model, and what each predicts
  • Why observer-ratings, not self-reports, better predict job success

Measuring Personality

Research shows personality tests genuinely help managers forecast who's best for a job — which is why they're used in hiring decisions. There are two main measurement approaches. Self-report surveys have individuals evaluate themselves (e.g. "I worry a lot about the future"). Observer-rating surveys have a colleague or another observer rate the person instead, sometimes with their knowledge and sometimes without.

MethodStrengthWeakness
Self-reportFast, scalable, and the person's own insight into themselvesVulnerable to impression management — people "fake good" when they know it affects a hiring decision
Observer-ratingIndependent of the subject's own self-presentationReflects only what's externally visible, which can miss internal traits
The counter-intuitive finding: self-report and observer-rating surveys are strongly correlated, but not perfectly — and the correlation is stronger for the more visible traits like Extraversion and Conscientiousness. Despite self-reports being more common, research suggests observer-ratings are the better predictor of job success. If you only trust what candidates say about themselves, you're using the weaker signal.

The Big Five Model

An impressive body of research supports five basic dimensions underlying most of the significant variation in human personality — and these dimensions do a genuinely good job predicting how people behave in real-life situations.

Module 04 / 06

The Dark Triad & Project-Manager Fit

Besides the Big Five, researchers have identified three socially undesirable traits that everyone carries to varying degrees — and which matter directly for how project teams actually function. Then: the practical question of fit, for the people you staff and for yourself.

What you'll learn
  • Machiavellianism, Narcissism, and Psychopathy — the Dark Triad, defined properly
  • What the evidence actually says each one predicts (and doesn't) at work
  • Person-organisation fit, and why the PM should evaluate their own fit too

The Dark Triad

With the exception of neuroticism, the Big Five traits are socially desirable — we're glad to score high on them. The Dark Triad is different: three traits we all carry in varying degrees, which researchers group together precisely because of their shared negative character, even though they don't always occur together in the same person.

The three traits of the Dark Triad
Everyone carries each of these to some degree — the Dark Triad describes a spectrum, not a diagnosis.

What the Evidence Actually Shows

  • Machiavellianism (high Mach): manipulates more, wins more, is persuaded less, and persuades others more than low-Mach people. High Machs act aggressively and engage in more counterproductive work behaviours — but Machiavellianism doesn't significantly predict overall job performance. They often win in the short term by manipulating others, then lose those gains long-term because they aren't well liked.
  • Narcissism: a sense of entitlement and arrogance, paired with a need for excessive admiration — and narcissists tend to be rated as less effective, despite (or because of) their self-confidence.
  • Psychopathy: findings are inconsistent on whether it correlates with job performance, but it's related to the use of hard influence tactics (threats, manipulation) and bullying behaviour. The cunning it can produce may help someone gain power in an organisation — but it tends to keep them from using that power toward healthy ends.
Why this matters for a PM: all three Dark Triad traits can look like confidence, decisiveness, or results-orientation in a first impression or a job interview — and all three predict worse long-run team outcomes than they promise up front. Weight observer-ratings (Module 3) more heavily than charm in high-stakes hiring, especially for roles with real authority over others.

Project-Manager Fit

In many real projects, staff get assigned to a team purely on the basis of availability — whoever's free gets slotted in. But a PM who understands person-organisation fit has a measurably better chance of success, because they can use that understanding to actively manage their team's composition and dynamics, rather than treating the roster as fixed.

Just as important: it's worth the PM evaluating their own fit, introspectively, with both the organisation and the type of project they're running. A PM who thrives running tightly-scoped, predictive construction projects may struggle — not from lack of skill, but from poor fit — when dropped into an ambiguous, fast-moving innovation project, and vice versa.

Module 05 / 06

Values: Terminal & Instrumental

Values are the deepest layer underneath attitudes and motivation — the basic convictions that shape what people treat as right, good, or desirable long before any specific attitude ever forms about a specific situation.

What you'll learn
  • What values are, and why they're unusually stable once formed
  • Terminal values (desirable end-states) vs. instrumental values (preferred means)
  • Why values matter for predicting attitudes, motivation, and perception

What Values Are, and Why They Stick

Values represent basic convictions that a specific mode of conduct or end-state of existence is personally or socially preferable to its opposite. Values lay the foundation for understanding people's attitudes and motivation, and they directly influence perception — they are, by definition, not neutral. They carry a judgmental element about what's right, good, or desirable.

Values are also unusually stable. A significant portion of the values we hold are established early — by parents, teachers, and friends — often taught in absolute, black-or-white terms (you were never taught to be "a little bit honest"). That absolute early learning is exactly what makes values so stable and enduring into adulthood.

Terminal vs. Instrumental Values

Terminal values refer to desirable end-states — the goals a person wants to achieve during their lifetime (prosperity and economic success, freedom, health and well-being, world peace, meaning in life). Instrumental values refer to the preferred modes of behaviour, or the means of achieving those terminal values (autonomy and self-reliance, personal discipline, kindness, goal-orientation).

Means and ends — an instrumental value chain toward a terminal value
Instrumental values are the "how"; terminal values are the "what for." The same instrumental value can serve several different terminal values at once.
🏁

Terminal Values

Examples: prosperity and economic success, freedom, health and well-being, world peace, meaning in life.

🧭

Instrumental Values

Examples: autonomy and self-reliance, personal discipline, kindness, goal-orientation.

Project application: when a conflict over "the right way to do this" keeps resurfacing even after the facts are settled, it's often not a disagreement about information at all — it's two people operating from different instrumental values (one prizes autonomy, the other prizes strict adherence to process) in service of the same terminal value (project success). Naming the underlying value, not just the surface disagreement, is usually what unlocks the conversation.
