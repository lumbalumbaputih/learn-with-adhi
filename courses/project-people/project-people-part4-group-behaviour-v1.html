Module 01 / 06

Defining & Classifying Groups

Every project runs on groups — but not all groups are the same kind of thing, and the difference between a "formal" and an "informal" group explains a lot of what actually drives behaviour on a team.

What you'll learn
  • The formal definition of a group — and why it requires interdependence, not just proximity
  • Formal groups vs. informal groups, and why informal groups matter just as much

What Counts as a Group

A group is defined as two or more individuals, interacting and interdependent, who have come together to achieve particular objectives. The word interdependent is doing real work here — a crowd of people waiting at a bus stop are not a group in this sense, because nothing one of them does depends on, or affects, what the others do. A project team is a group precisely because each member's work actually depends on the others'.

Formal Groups vs. Informal Groups

Formal groups are those defined by the organisation's structure, with designated work assignments establishing specific tasks. In a formal group, the behaviours members should engage in are stipulated by, and directed toward, organisational goals — a six-person project delivery team assembled on a charter is a formal group.

Informal groups are neither formally structured nor organisationally determined. They're natural formations that appear in response to the need for social contact — three team members from different workstreams who regularly grab coffee together are an informal group. These interactions are not written into anyone's job description, but they deeply affect behaviour and performance on the formal team all the same.

Same project, two overlapping group structures
The formal structure is drawn by the organisation. The informal one is drawn by the people in it — and both shape what actually happens on the project.
Why this distinction matters: a PM who only manages the formal structure — the charter, the RACI, the reporting lines — is managing half the team. Information, trust, and early warning of problems travel through the informal network just as often as through the formal one, and sometimes faster.
Quick Check – Module 1

Module 02 / 06

Why People Form Groups

Social identity theory explains something project managers feel constantly but rarely name: why "our team" vs. "their team" becomes emotionally real so fast, and why people defend group membership even when it costs them something individually.

What you'll learn
  • Social identity theory's four drivers of group membership
  • Why high-status groups attract stronger identification
  • How group identity reduces uncertainty about "who we are"

Social Identity Theory

Social identity theory considers when and why individuals come to see themselves as members of a group. Four characteristics make a social identity matter to a person:

🪞

Similarity

We identify more strongly with people who are like us.

🔀

Distinctiveness

We're drawn to identities that mark us as different from other groups.

🏆

Status

We use identities to raise our self-esteem — so we're more likely to identify with high-status groups.

🧭

Uncertainty Reduction

Membership tells us who we are and how we're meant to behave, reducing ambiguity.

Project application: a project explicitly branded and resourced as the organisation's flagship initiative attracts stronger voluntary identification than an unglamorous maintenance project — not because the work itself differs in difficulty, but because status feeds the self-esteem driver of social identity. Naming and visibly backing a project changes how much people identify with it, independent of the work.
The same four drivers that bond a group also separate it from others
The four drivers don't just build "us" — the act of building "us" simultaneously defines "them," which is why rivalry needs no deliberate trigger.

Identity Cuts Both Ways

The same forces that bind a project team together also build walls between it and other teams. If "our team" is distinctive and high-status, by definition "their team" is something else — which is precisely why inter-team rivalry (engineering vs. design, project vs. operations) emerges so naturally, without anyone deliberately starting a conflict. We'll return to this directly when we cover intergroup conflict in Part 9.

Module 03 / 06

Stages of Group Development

Bruce Tuckman's model describes a predictable sequence every group tends to move through — and knowing which stage a team is in changes what a PM should actually do about the friction they're seeing.

What you'll learn
  • Tuckman's five stages: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning
  • Why "storming" is a predictable stage, not a sign the team has failed
  • How the model itself evolved after its original publication

The Five Stages

Tuckman's model (1965) describes groups moving through five predictable stages: forming (members get oriented, test boundaries, and depend on a leader for direction), storming (conflict emerges over roles, authority, and direction — genuine intragroup conflict), norming (close relationships develop, cohesion emerges, roles and norms stabilise), performing (the group is fully functional, energy has moved from getting-to-know-you dynamics to the actual task), and adjourning (for temporary groups like project teams, wrapping up activities and disbanding).

The five stages, as a project team's energy curve
The dip at "storming" is normal and predictable — the mistake is treating it as team failure instead of a stage to manage through.
A nuance worth knowing: Tuckman's original 1965 paper actually proposed only four stages — forming, storming, norming, performing. "Adjourning" was added over a decade later, in Tuckman & Jensen's 1977 follow-up paper, once researchers recognised that temporary groups (like most project teams) need an explicit stage for winding down. It's a good reminder that even famous, widely-taught models get revised after publication.

Module 04 / 06

The Psychological Contract

Beneath every written employment agreement sits an unwritten one — a set of mutual expectations about what each side owes the other. Understanding which kind your project team operates under changes what you can reasonably ask of them.

What you'll learn
  • What a psychological contract is, and why it's never written down
  • Relational contracts vs. transactional contracts

Mutual Expectations, Unwritten

A psychological contract captures the mutual expectations of commitment between an employer and employee — a two-way exchange of perceived promises and obligations that no one signs, but everyone operates under. When either side feels the other has broken this unwritten deal, trust erodes fast, often faster than when a written contract term is breached, precisely because the psychological contract was never explicit enough to negotiate or clarify in the first place.

Relational vs. Transactional Contracts

Relational contracts are usually longer-term, open-ended relationships within unitary (single) organisations, built on an exchange of loyalty, trust, and support. Transactional contracts are short-term relationships within pluralistic (mixed) organisational contexts, characterised by mutual self-interest rather than loyalty.

Relational ContractTransactional Contract
Time horizonLonger-term, open-endedShort-term, defined
Organisational contextUnitary (single) organisationPluralistic (mixed) context
What's exchangedLoyalty, trust, supportMutual self-interest
Typical exampleA core, long-tenured employeeA contractor hired for one project phase
What flows each way, under each kind of contract
Neither contract is "better" — they're calibrated to a different time horizon and context. The trouble starts when a PM applies relational expectations to a transactional relationship, or vice versa.
A common PM mistake: treating a contractor or short-term specialist (whose psychological contract is transactional) as if they owe the same loyalty-driven discretionary effort as a long-tenured core team member (whose contract is relational) — and then feeling "betrayed" when they don't deliver it. The mismatch is in the expectation, not in the contractor's conduct.

Module 05 / 06

Groupthink & Groupshift

Groups don't just combine individual judgment — they can systematically distort it, in two distinct and well-documented ways.

What you'll learn
  • Groupthink: how the drive for consensus overrides realistic appraisal
  • Groupshift: why groups push initial positions toward extremes

Groupthink

Groupthink is a phenomenon where the norm for consensus overrides the realistic appraisal of alternative courses of action. A cohesive team under pressure to agree can suppress doubts, self-censor dissent, and converge on a decision that no individual member, questioned privately, would actually endorse.

The warning signs: a project steering committee that moves suspiciously fast to consensus on a risky decision, where dissenting voices go quiet rather than being argued down, is a groupthink red flag — not a sign of strong alignment. Genuine alignment survives someone playing devil's advocate; groupthink doesn't.

Groupshift

Groupshift describes how group members tend to exaggerate their initial positions when discussing a decision as a group, producing a measurable shift in risk between what the group ultimately decides and what an individual member would have decided alone. That shift can go either way — toward greater conservatism, or toward greater risk — but it's rarely neutral.

Groupthink vs. groupshift — two distinct distortions
Groupthink compresses a range of private views into one artificial consensus. Groupshift pushes a single view further toward an extreme. They're different mechanisms, and a team can suffer from either, or both.
