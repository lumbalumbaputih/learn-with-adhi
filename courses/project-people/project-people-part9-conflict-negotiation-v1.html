Module 01 / 05

Views on Conflict

Not all conflict is a project management failure. Whether conflict helps or hurts your project depends entirely on what kind it is — and pretending it should never happen at all is itself a well-documented mistake.

What you'll learn
  • The traditional view of conflict, versus the interactionist view
  • Task, relationship, and process conflict — which ones can actually help
  • The three loci of conflict: dyadic, intragroup, and intergroup

Two Views on Conflict

ViewCore claimSources named
TraditionalAll conflict is harmful and must be avoidedPoor communication, lack of openness, failure to respond to needs
InteractionistConflict is a positive force, necessary for a group to perform effectivelyConflict can be functional OR dysfunctional, depending on type and intensity

The interactionist view doesn't claim all conflict is good — it recognises two distinct forms, and intensity matters as much as type.

📋

Task Conflict

Disagreement over the content and goals of the work. At low-to-moderate levels, this is functional — it surfaces better ideas and clarifies ambiguity. But it can evolve into relationship conflict if left unmanaged.

💥

Relationship Conflict

Conflict based on interpersonal friction. Almost always dysfunctional — it rarely produces anything useful and tends to damage trust.

⚙️

Process Conflict

Disagreement over how the work gets done. Functional at low levels (surfacing a better way of working); corrosive if it drags on.

The Three Loci of Conflict

Where conflict occurs matters as much as what kind it is. There are three basic loci: dyadic conflict between two people, intragroup conflict within a single team, and intergroup conflict between two separate groups or teams.

The three loci of conflict, nested
Intergroup conflict connects directly back to Social Identity Theory (Part 4) — the same in-group/out-group dynamics that explain why people join groups also explain why groups clash.
Why the distinction matters on a project: a PM who suppresses ALL conflict, following the traditional view, loses the genuine benefit of low-level task conflict (better ideas, surfaced ambiguity) along with the harm of relationship conflict. The skill isn't eliminating conflict — it's telling functional task/process conflict apart from dysfunctional relationship conflict, and managing each differently.

Module 02 / 05

The Conflict Process & Five Handling Intentions

Conflict doesn't appear out of nowhere — it follows a traceable process, and once it does appear, everyone instinctively defaults to one of five handling styles. Knowing all five means you can choose deliberately instead of defaulting.

What you'll learn
  • Why attending to antecedent conditions early prevents poor conflict outcomes later
  • The five conflict-handling intentions, mapped on assertiveness and cooperativeness
  • How to read your own and others' default conflict style

The Conflict Process — Working Backward

Taking a "backward pass" through the conflict process shows that attending to antecedent conditions — the underlying conditions present before any conflict ever becomes visible — is crucial. Time invested at the very beginning of a project (clarifying goals, roles, and resource allocation) is what prevents conflict from escalating into something destructive later. By the time a conflict is visible, the cheapest intervention point has usually already passed.

Five Conflict-Handling Intentions

Once conflict is underway, people respond with one of five intentions, which can be mapped along two dimensions: how assertive they are about their own interests, and how cooperative they are about the other party's.

The five conflict-handling intentions, plotted
No single intention is "best" — each is the right call under specific conditions of power, time, and how much the issue matters to each side (Module 3).
Definitions, precisely: accommodating = a willingness to place the opponent's interests above your own. Avoiding = a desire to withdraw from or suppress the conflict. Compromising = each party gives up something. Competing = satisfying your own interests regardless of impact on the other. Collaborating = each party desires to fully satisfy the concerns of all parties.
Quick Check – Module 2

A team member consistently backs down from any disagreement, changes the subject, or leaves the room rather than engage — even on issues that matter to the project. Which conflict-handling intention is this?

Module 03 / 05

Choosing the Right Technique

None of the five intentions is universally correct. Which one to actually use depends on two practical factors — and Sutterfield et al.'s research gives a concrete, type-by-type recommendation for project settings.

What you'll learn
  • The two factors that determine which conflict technique fits a situation
  • Sutterfield et al.'s technique recommendations by conflict type
  • Why "it depends" is the only honest answer — and what it depends on

Two Factors That Decide the Technique

  • Power and influence held by each party. If you have very little power and the other party has a lot, "hard" techniques like competing/domination are risky — softer techniques (compromise, accommodation) are the prudent choice.
  • Perceived criticality of the issue to each party. If the issue matters intensely to you but barely to the other side, you have more room to push; if it's critical to them and not to you, accommodation may cost you little and buy a lot of goodwill.

Sutterfield et al.'s Technique-by-Conflict-Type Guidance

Conflict typeRecommended techniqueDepends on
Relationship conflictCompromise or collaborateWho's involved — e.g. collaborate with a project sponsor; compromise or collaborate with another contractor's manager
Task conflictCompete, compromise, or collaboratePower/influence held, and time available — little power favours competing defensively; lots of power + no time favours compromise; otherwise collaborate
Process conflictCompete or compromiseFlexibility of the process — mandated steps favour competing (hold the line); some leeway favours compromise
The two factors combined, as a decision aid
This grid combines the module's two named factors (power/influence, and issue criticality) into one decision aid — it's a synthesis for teaching purposes, not a direct slide from the source lecture.
The nuance worth sitting with: notice collaborating never appears as the recommendation for process conflict in the table above. When the "how" is mandated by regulation or contract, there's often genuinely no room to fully satisfy everyone's preferred process — competing (holding the line) or compromising are the realistic options, even though collaborating sounds nicer in theory.

Module 04 / 05

Negotiation: Distributive & Integrative

Negotiation happens whenever two or more parties decide how to allocate scarce resources — and on a project, that's constantly. There are exactly two fundamentally different ways to approach it, and picking the wrong one for the situation leaves value on the table.

What you'll learn
  • Distributive bargaining vs. integrative bargaining, and the strategy for each
  • The three negotiation roles a PM actually fulfils
  • PMI's five things every project manager should know about negotiation

Distributive vs. Integrative Bargaining

Negotiation occurs when two or more parties decide how to allocate scarce resources — establishing a process through which some conflicts get resolved.

Two bargaining strategies, two different "pies"
Distributive bargaining splits a fixed amount; integrative bargaining first tries to make the total bigger before dividing it.
DistributiveIntegrative
AssumptionAny gain by one party is a loss to the otherA mutually beneficial, "win-win" outcome is achievable
Effective strategyMake the first offer, make it aggressive (power + anchoring bias); revealing a deadline speeds the other side's concessionsBargain in teams (more ideas generated); put more issues on the table; focus on underlying interests, not stated positions

PMI's 5 Things Every Project Manager Should Know About Negotiation

  1. Project negotiations are usually ongoing — not a single, one-shot event.
  2. There are two types: distributive and integrative (above).
  3. A PM fulfils three negotiation roles: negotiator (party to the dispute), mediator (helping two other parties reach agreement), and arbitrator (empowered to impose a binding decision).
  4. Negotiation involves three stages: planning, engagement, and closing.
  5. Project success can genuinely be influenced by negotiation quality — negotiation among team members reaches consensus and builds trust and harmony.
Project application: most negotiation advice assumes a single deal, once. Project negotiation is the opposite — you'll negotiate with the same stakeholders repeatedly across months. That changes the calculus: an aggressive, win-at-all-costs distributive tactic that "wins" one negotiation can poison every negotiation after it. Integrative habits compound; distributive wins often don't.
