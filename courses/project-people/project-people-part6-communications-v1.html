Module 01 / 06

Why We Communicate

Communication is the transfer and understanding of meaning — transfer alone isn't enough. A message that arrives but isn't understood hasn't actually been communicated. Every instance of communication on a project serves one (or more) of exactly four functions.

What you'll learn
  • Why "transfer of meaning" isn't the same as "transfer of understanding"
  • The four functions of communication: control, motivation, emotional expression, information
  • Why none of the four is "more important" — and what that means in practice

The Four Functions

Almost every communication that happens inside a group or organisation performs one or more of four functions. None of them outranks the others — a healthy team needs all four operating at once.

The four functions, radiating from one act of communication
All four functions fire constantly, often in the same conversation — a single stand-up meeting can control, motivate, vent feelings, and transmit decision-relevant information all at once.

Control Isn't Only Formal

Organisations control behaviour through authority hierarchies and formal guidelines — job descriptions, reporting lines, required status updates. But control also happens informally: when a work group teases or socially pressures a member who's "producing too much" and making the rest of the team look bad, that's communication performing a control function too, with no manager involved at all.

Project scenario: a team member silently resents being left out of a decision — but says nothing. Nothing was transferred, so by definition nothing was communicated, however strongly they felt it. The fix isn't a better feeling on their end; it's an actual act of communication (even just naming the frustration out loud) that gets understood by the other side.

Module 02 / 06

The Communication Process — and Why It Isn't Linear

Classic communication theory describes eight components running sender to receiver. Real communication adds two complications the clean diagram leaves out: interruptions, and the context both people are standing in.

What you'll learn
  • The 8 components of the communication process, in order
  • Where "noise" sits in the model, and why feedback is what closes the loop
  • Why real communication is interrupted and context-dependent, not a clean pipe

The Eight Components

Before communication can happen, it needs a purpose — a message to convey between a sender and a receiver. The sender encodes the message (converts it into a symbolic form — words, a chart, a gesture) and passes it through a channel (a medium) to the receiver, who decodes it. The receiver then gives the sender feedback, which lets the sender know whether the message landed correctly. Noise is the broad term for everything that disturbs this process along the way.

The communication process, with noise and the feedback loop
Eight components: sender, encoding, message, channel, decoding, receiver, noise, feedback. Without feedback, the sender has no way to know decoding actually succeeded.

Not Quite So Linear

The eight-component diagram is a useful skeleton, but real communication rarely runs that cleanly in one direction. Two things complicate it in practice:

  • Interruptions. Real conversations are interrupted, restarted, and talked over — meaning gets built collaboratively in overlapping turns, not delivered in a single clean transmission.
  • Context. The same message decodes differently depending on where, when, and under what pressure it's received — a status update read calmly at a desk lands differently than the identical words skimmed in a five-second gap between two meetings.
It depends — on timing as much as wording: a PM who has carefully encoded the "perfect" message can still have it land badly if the channel or the moment is wrong. Sending a nuanced piece of critical feedback by instant message, right before a stand-up the receiver is late for, all but guarantees it gets decoded through the lens of stress and rush rather than the content itself.

Module 03 / 06

Modes of Communication

Oral, written, and non-verbal communication each carry meaning differently — and the famous "55/38/7" statistic about how much of a message is words versus tone versus body language is one of the most widely cited and widely misapplied numbers in management training.

What you'll learn
  • The three modes: oral, written, non-verbal, and what each is best suited for
  • The 55/38/7 rule — and the important limits on what it actually measured

The Three Modes

ModeIncludesBest suited for
OralMeetings, videoconferencing, phone callsFast back-and-forth, nuance, reading reactions in real time
WrittenMemos, letters, email, instant messaging, periodicalsA durable record, precision, something the receiver can revisit
Non-verbalBody movements, tone/emphasis, facial expressions, physical distanceSignalling feeling and attitude — often involuntarily
The 55/38/7 split, as it's commonly (and over-broadly) cited
The ratio everyone quotes — but it was measured under one specific condition, not as a universal law of communication.
It depends — and in this case, a lot: this 55/38/7 split comes from research into communicating feelings and attitudes, specifically in cases where a listener had to judge sincerity when the verbal and non-verbal signals conflicted (e.g. someone saying "I'm fine" in a flat, unconvincing tone). It was never meant as a universal rule that words only carry 7% of the meaning of every message you send — a written risk register, a cost estimate, or a technical spec is almost entirely "words," and treating this split as gospel for all communication badly misreads the original research. Use it as a reminder that tone and body language carry real weight when feelings are involved, not as a formula.

Module 04 / 06

Persuasion: Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Aristotle's three appeals are over two thousand years old and still describe exactly how modern stakeholders get persuaded. Layered on top: which of two mental "processing modes" your audience is using determines whether facts or feelings will actually move them.

What you'll learn
  • Aristotle's three modes of persuasion: ethos, pathos, logos
  • Automatic vs. controlled processing, and the four factors that predict which one your audience uses

Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Aristotle identified three distinct ways a speaker persuades an audience: ethos (an appeal to ethics — convincing via the authority or credibility of the persuader, e.g. an expert or a respected figure), pathos (an appeal to emotion — convincing via an impassioned plea or a compelling story), and logos (an appeal to logic — convincing with facts, figures, and reasoned argument).

The three classical appeals
A business case with zero logos (no numbers) or zero ethos (an unknown, untrusted presenter) rarely survives contact with a steering committee, however moving the pathos.

Automatic vs. Controlled Processing

Automatic processing is a relatively superficial consideration of evidence, relying on heuristics — it's fast, low-effort, and vulnerable to tricks like a catchy jingle or a glamorous photo. Controlled processing is a detailed, effortful consideration of facts, figures, and logic — it takes more energy, but it's much harder to fool.

Four factors predict which mode an audience will use:

  • Interest level — the more a decision will actually affect someone's life, the more likely they are to engage controlled processing (think: choosing where to live vs. which soft drink to buy).
  • Prior knowledge — well-informed people have already thought through the arguments, so they default to controlled processing and are harder to sway with superficial claims.
  • Personality — people high in "need for cognition" are more naturally drawn to evidence and facts; those lower in it lean on intuition and emotion.
  • Message characteristics (richness) — lean channels with little room for interaction (a fast TV ad) push the audience toward automatic processing; rich channels (a long, detailed article) invite controlled processing.
The practical takeaway: match your persuasive approach to the processing mode your audience is actually likely to use. For a disengaged, poorly-informed audience consuming your message through a lean channel, lean on ethos and pathos — emotion-laden framing and credible, positive associations. For an engaged, well-informed audience with a rich channel (a detailed briefing document), lean on logos — rational argument and evidence carry the day.

Module 05 / 06

Barriers to Effective Communication

Eight distinct barriers can distort or block communication on any given project — four about how information gets shaped and received, and four more specific, surprisingly common failure modes worth naming individually.

What you'll learn
  • Filtering, selective perception, information overload, and emotions as distortion points
  • Language, silence, communication apprehension, and lying — and how common each actually is

Four Barriers Along the Channel

Where distortion enters the communication process
Filtering happens on the sender's side; selective perception, overload, and emotions happen on the receiver's.
  • Filtering — a sender manipulating information so the receiver sees it more favourably (a manager telling their boss what they think the boss wants to hear).
  • Selective perception — receivers interpret what they see and hear based on their own needs, motivations, experience, and background, projecting their own expectations into the message.
  • Information overload — a condition where information inflow exceeds an individual's processing capacity.
  • Emotions — how a receiver feels when they get a message shapes how they interpret it; people in positive moods tend to accept communications more at face value, while people in negative moods scrutinise them more closely.

Language

Even within the same language, the same words mean different things to different people — age and context are two of the biggest sources of this difference.

🤐

Silence

Easy to overlook precisely because it's an absence — but withholding communication about significant issues is common, and genuinely problematic.

😰

Communication Apprehension

Undue tension and anxiety about oral and/or written communication — severe enough in some people that they'll use a slower memo rather than a faster phone call just to avoid a live exchange.

🎭

Lying

Outright misrepresentation of information — and people genuinely differ in where they personally draw the line on what counts as "a lie" at all.

Why silence is the most dangerous barrier on this list: filtering, overload, and apprehension at least leave some trace. Silence leaves none — a PM who doesn't actively create space for dissent can run an entire project believing alignment exists, right up until the moment a withheld concern becomes a missed deadline or a blown budget.
