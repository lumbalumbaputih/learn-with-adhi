Defining Motivation & Maslow's Hierarchy

Motivation isn't one thing — it's the combination of three measurable dimensions of effort, and the first theory to explain where that effort comes from is still the most recognisable one in management.

What you'll learn
  • Motivation's three dimensions: intensity, direction, persistence
  • Maslow's five needs and the hierarchy's key assumption
  • Lower-order vs. higher-order needs

Defining Motivation

Motivation refers to the processes that account for an individual's intensity, direction, and persistence of effort toward attaining a work-related goal.

  • Intensity describes how hard a person tries.
  • Direction — high-intensity effort only helps if it's channelled toward something that actually benefits the work; otherwise the effort isn't beneficial to performance.
  • Persistence measures how long a person can sustain effort. Motivated people stay with a task long enough to actually achieve the goal.

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Maslow (1943) proposed a hierarchy of five needs: physiological, safety, social, esteem, and self-actualisation. As each need becomes substantially satisfied, the next one up the hierarchy becomes dominant. Lower-order needs (physiological, safety) are satisfied externally. Higher-order needs (social, esteem, self-actualisation) are satisfied internally.

The hierarchy's key — and most criticised — assumption: individuals cannot move up to the next level until all needs at the current level are substantially satisfied, meaning people are assumed to climb the hierarchy strictly in order.

Maslow's five needs, lower-order vs. higher-order
The theory's strict assumption: a person won't be motivated by esteem needs while physiological or safety needs remain substantially unmet.
Project application: a team member worried about job security after a round of layoffs (a safety need) will be hard to motivate with "growth opportunity" talk (an esteem/self-actualisation appeal) until the safety concern is genuinely addressed — Maslow's hierarchy predicts the lower-order concern has to be settled first, not talked past.

Module 02 / 06

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Herzberg's (1966) most important — and most counter-intuitive — claim: satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of the same scale. They're driven by two entirely separate sets of factors.

What you'll learn
  • Hygiene factors (extrinsic) vs. motivators (intrinsic)
  • Why removing dissatisfaction doesn't create satisfaction
  • The theory's main critiques

Two Separate Scales, Not One

The conventional view treats satisfaction and dissatisfaction as two ends of a single scale. Herzberg's two-factor theory says otherwise: hygiene factors (extrinsic — pay, policies, working conditions, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction when present, but don't create satisfaction on their own. Motivators (intrinsic — recognition, responsibility, achievement, growth) are what actually create satisfaction.

Two independent scales, not one continuum
Raising pay (hygiene) can take dissatisfaction away — it cannot, by itself, manufacture genuine satisfaction. Only motivators do that.

The Critique

  • The theory assumes happy employees automatically produce more — a link that isn't reliably supported.
  • What motivates one individual can genuinely de-motivate another.
  • It doesn't account for individual personality differences that could produce a different response to the exact same motivator or hygiene factor.
The common PM mistake: responding to a disengaged team by improving only hygiene factors — a bigger budget for snacks, nicer office chairs, more flexible hours — and being surprised when engagement doesn't move. Hygiene factors can remove active dissatisfaction, but genuine motivation (and the discretionary effort that comes with it) requires the intrinsic motivators: real recognition, real responsibility, real growth.
Quick Check – Module 2

Module 03 / 06

McClelland's Learned Needs Theory

McClelland (1961) argues people are driven by three learned needs, present in different strengths in different people — and one of his most surprising findings concerns who makes the most effective executive.

What you'll learn
  • The three needs: achievement, affiliation, power
  • Personalised vs. socialised power
  • The theory's main critique — can needs really be "learned" in adulthood?

The Three Needs

🎯

Need for Achievement

The need to reach goals and take personal responsibility. People high in this need want reasonably challenging goals — not trivially easy ones, and not impossible ones.

🤝

Need for Affiliation

A desire to seek approval, conform to others' wishes, and avoid conflict. Counter-intuitively, effective executives tend to have a LOWER need for social approval, not a higher one.

⚡

Need for Power

A desire to control one's environment. Splits into personalised power (control for its own sake) vs. socialised power (control used in service of a group or cause).

Three independent needs — everyone has their own profile
Bar lengths here are illustrative of one hypothetical profile — the theory's point is that each need varies independently, with no fixed "ideal" mix, and power itself splits further into two very different uses.
The surprising finding: a high need for affiliation sounds like a leadership asset — being well-liked, seeking consensus — but the research specifically finds effective executives tend to have a lower need for social approval. A leader who needs to be liked by everyone will struggle to make the unpopular calls a project sometimes requires.

The Critique

McClelland's main premise — that these needs can be learned by adults — has attracted major criticism. It conflicts with a large body of literature finding that motives are normally acquired in childhood and are difficult to alter once a person reaches adulthood. Treat the theory as a useful lens for understanding existing individual differences in need strength, not as a guaranteed training programme for installing new needs in adults.

Module 04 / 06

Self-Efficacy

Bandura's (1977) self-efficacy is one of the best predictors of future performance we have — and it's built from four specific, identifiable sources, not just innate confidence.

What you'll learn
  • What self-efficacy is, and how it differs from self-esteem
  • The four sources of self-efficacy
  • Why negative verbal feedback hurts efficacy more than positive feedback helps it

Self-Efficacy, Defined

Self-efficacy is an individual's own feeling that they can complete a specific task. It is not the same as self-esteem (a person's general degree of liking or disliking themselves) — self-efficacy is task-specific, not a global self-evaluation. It is one of the single best predictors of future performance available.

The Four Sources of Self-Efficacy

Four inputs feeding one output: a person's efficacy belief
All four sources can move the same efficacy belief in either direction — none of them are fixed traits.
  • Mastery experiences — your own personal history of success or failure at similar tasks. Good past performance builds a positive sense of your own ability.
  • Vicarious experiences — watching someone similar to you succeed at a threatening or difficult task raises your own expectation that you can acquire the same skill, often by imitation.
  • Verbal persuasion — feedback that encourages someone to attempt and complete a task. "You can do it" is the most common form.
  • Physiological state — anxiety, a racing heart, sweating. These physical symptoms reflect (and in turn affect) a person's own perception of their efficacy for the task at hand.
An asymmetry worth remembering: negative verbal messages have an even greater effect on lowering efficacy expectations than positive messages have on raising them. A single discouraging comment from a respected source can undo several encouraging ones — which is a strong practical argument for being careful with critical feedback around a task someone is still building confidence in.

Module 05 / 06

Four-Drive, Reinforcement & Expectancy Theory

Three more lenses on motivation, each asking a different question: what innate drives do all humans share? How does reinforcement shape repeated behaviour? And what has to be true, in someone's own head, before they'll put in effort at all?

What you'll learn
  • The Four-Drive theory's four innate, independent drives
  • Reinforcement theory's core claim about behaviour and consequences
  • Expectancy theory's effort → performance → reward → goal chain

Four-Drive Theory

Research identifies four underlying drives: to Acquire & Achieve, to Bond & Belong, to be Challenged & Comprehend, and to Define & Defend. Three defining features: they're innate and hardwired (everyone has all four), independent of each other (no hierarchy — unlike Maslow's model), and a complete set (no major drive is left out). Most organisations focus heavily on the Acquire & Achieve drive (base pay, incentives) while under-investing in the other three — which is exactly where a lot of unrealised engagement sits.

DriveWhat it looks like satisfied
Acquire & AchieveFair pay, recognition, advancement — the drive most organisations already address
Bond & BelongGenuine team connection, inclusion, a sense of "my people"
Challenge & ComprehendMeaningful, non-trivial problems; the chance to understand, not just execute
Define & DefendClear values, a defensible sense of what the work stands for and why

Reinforcement Theory

Skinner's reinforcement theory argues behaviour is a function of its consequences. Positive consequences (e.g. a financial reward) encourage a behaviour; negative consequences discourage it. Three assumptions underpin it: behaviour is environmentally caused; behaviour can be modified by controlling its consequences; and reinforced behaviour tends to be repeated. Praise, nagging, public reprimand, and withholding a bonus are all, in this framework, reinforcement mechanisms — whether used deliberately or not.

Expectancy Theory

Vroom's expectancy theory argues the strength of a tendency to act a certain way depends on the strength of the expectation of a given outcome, and the attractiveness of that outcome. In practice: a team member exerts high effort when they believe (1) effort will lead to good performance, (2) good performance will lead to a reward (bonus, promotion, recognition), and (3) that reward will satisfy their own personal goals. Break any one link in that chain, and the whole motivational effect collapses — even if the other two links are perfectly intact.

Expectancy theory's chain — and where it breaks
All three links have to hold for expectancy theory's motivational effect to actually show up in behaviour.
