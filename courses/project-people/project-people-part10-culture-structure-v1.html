The Seven Core Characteristics of Culture

Organisational culture is a system of shared meaning held by members that distinguishes one organisation from another. It isn't a slogan on a wall — it's a composite picture built from seven measurable characteristics, each one a continuum from low to high.

What you'll learn
  • The seven characteristics that together describe any organisation's culture
  • Why each one is a continuum, not a yes/no category
  • How appraising an organisation on all seven produces its "composite picture"

Seven Characteristics, Each a Continuum

Appraising an organisation on these seven characteristics gives a composite picture of its culture — and that picture becomes the basis for the shared understanding members have about "how things are done here," and how they're supposed to behave.

Same seven characteristics, two very different organisations
Neither profile is "better" in the abstract — each is a legitimate, coherent culture fitted to a very different operating reality.
Why this matters for a PM: these seven characteristics aren't just a description of "the organisation" in the abstract — a project team, especially a cross-functional one, often inherits a blend of its parent departments' positions on each continuum. A project combining an aggressive sales culture with a detail-obsessed engineering culture will feel the friction on exactly these seven dimensions before anyone names it as a "culture clash."

Module 02 / 05

The Competing Values Framework

Cameron & Quinn (2005) compress culture down to two dimensions, producing four named types: Clan, Adhocracy, Market, and Hierarchy. There is no universally "best" quadrant — only the one that fits an organisation's actual strategy and moment.

What you'll learn
  • The two axes that generate the framework: internal↔external, stability↔flexibility
  • What Clan, Adhocracy, Market, and Hierarchy cultures each prioritise
  • Why "the right culture" is a fit question, not a ranking

Two Axes, Four Types

The framework's first dimension is orientation: internal (focused inward — development, collaboration, integration, coordination) versus external (focused outward — the market, competitors, the latest technology, what customers want). The second dimension is stability (clear structures, planning, budgets, reliability — assumes reality can be known and controlled) versus flexibility (a flexible way of organising that adapts quickly to changing circumstances).

The Competing Values Framework, as a 2×2
Clan and Market sit on opposite corners (internal+flexible vs. external+stable); Adhocracy and Hierarchy sit on the other diagonal — hence "competing" values.

Diagnosing and Changing Organizational Culture
It depends, on purpose: the right culture is whichever one fits the organisation's own direction and strategy, and the challenges of its particular moment. A Market culture is exactly right for a firm fighting for share in a commoditised industry — and exactly wrong for an early-stage R&D lab that needs Adhocracy's tolerance for failed experiments. There's no universal ranking across the four quadrants.

Module 03 / 05

Why Culture Matters, and How It Forms

Culture isn't decorative — it does real organisational work. And it doesn't appear from nowhere: it traces back to a specific, traceable chain starting with the founder.

What you'll learn
  • The five functions culture performs inside an organisation
  • Why a strong culture measurably improves performance and retention
  • The chain from founder philosophy to a sustained culture

Why Culture Matters

🧭

Boundary-Defining

Creates distinctions between this organisation and others — part of what makes it recognisably itself.

🪪

Identity-Conveying

Gives members a sense of social identity — "who we are" by virtue of belonging here.

🤝

Commitment-Facilitating

Makes it easier for members to identify with something larger than their own immediate self-interest.

🧷

Social Glue

Shared standards of behaviour (norms) that hold the organisation together without needing a rule for everything.

🎛️

Controlling Mechanism

Shapes and guides members' attitudes and behaviour — often more powerfully than any written policy.

Factual example: "the Chevron way" is described as a culture dedicated to safety, supporting employees, and team members looking out for each other — a strong culture doesn't require ping-pong tables or free beer; simply giving employees a genuine sense of safety and mutual care can be enough. A strong culture — where members agree on and care intensely about shared values — improves performance by motivating employees and coordinating their behaviour toward a shared vision, and it attracts and retains better talent.

How Culture Forms

An organisation's culture doesn't appear from thin air, and once established, it rarely fades. It follows a specific chain:

Module 04 / 05

Dominant Culture, Subcultures & Project Culture

Most large organisations don't have one uniform culture — they have a dominant culture plus numerous subcultures. And a project team, temporary as it is, develops its own project culture on top of all of it.

What you'll learn
  • Dominant culture vs. subcultures, and why both exist simultaneously
  • How a subculture clash can blindside management
  • Project culture, specifically, as its own layer (Zuo 2008)

Dominant Culture & Subcultures

A dominant culture expresses the core values shared by a majority of an organisation's members — when people refer to "an organisation's culture" in general, this is what they mean. Subcultures are mini-cultures within the organisation, typically defined by department or geography, which can include the dominant culture's core values plus additional values unique to that group (e.g. a purchasing department's own professional norms layered on top of the company-wide culture).

One dominant culture, several subcultures nested inside it
Each subculture inherits the dominant culture's core values and layers its own department-specific values on top.
When a subculture clash blindsides management: in March 2018, United Airlines replaced small, guaranteed quarterly bonuses for every employee with a lottery that paid spectacularly to only a handful of people. Management thought it was an exciting improvement; employee anger and frustration hit the media within a day, and the program was pulled. The lesson: what reads as a great idea from the dominant culture's vantage point can appall the subculture actually living with the policy day to day.

Project Culture

Zuo (2008) defines project culture as the shared values, basic assumptions, and beliefs that the participants involved in a project hold — and it's this shared set of assumptions that determines how they process the project and relate to each other within the project environment. A project team is, in effect, a temporary subculture of its own, assembled fresh for each project and shaped by whichever parent subcultures its members are drawn from.
